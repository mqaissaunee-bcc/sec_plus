Data Protection, Resilience, and Recovery

Data types and classification, sovereignty and protection methods, high availability, backup strategy against recovery objectives, and the site and testing decisions that make continuity real.

Progress: 0% complete

Overview

[OBJECTIVES] By the end of this module you will be able to classify data by type and sensitivity and select handling requirements accordingly; explain data sovereignty and geographic restriction; compare high availability approaches including load balancing and clustering; design a backup strategy against stated recovery objectives; and choose between hot, warm, and cold sites and the exercises that test them.

This module completes Domain 3 and covers the two halves of protecting what an organization holds. Objective 3.3 asks how data is classified and guarded. Objective 3.4 asks how systems survive failure and how quickly they come back.

These pair naturally because they answer opposite halves of the same question. Data protection is largely about confidentiality and integrity; resilience and recovery are entirely about availability. Together they complete the triad from Module 1, which is why this module reads as a summation of Domain 3 rather than a new topic.

What carries over from earlier modules

Several protection methods named in objective 3.3 were covered in depth in Module 2 - encryption, hashing, tokenization, masking, and obfuscation. They appear here as items in a selection problem rather than as mechanisms to explain.

The shift in question is worth noticing, because it is the shift that defines Domain 3. Module 2 asked how tokenization works. This module asks when you would choose it over masking, and the answer depends on whether the original value must ever be recoverable. If you need the mechanism again, reread Module 2 rather than relearning it.

Two numbers that govern this whole module

Recovery decisions are driven by two objectives, and almost every question in the second half of this module turns on them.

ObjectiveQuestion it answersDetermined by
RPO - recovery point objectiveHow much data can we afford to lose?Backup frequency
RTO - recovery time objectiveHow long can we afford to be down?Recovery method and site readiness

Keeping these apart is the single highest-yield distinction here. RPO looks backward from the incident and is set by how often you back up. RTO looks forward from the incident and is set by how fast you can restore. An organization backing up nightly has a 24-hour RPO no matter how quickly it can restore, and an organization with instant replication still has a poor RTO if nobody has tested the failover.

These are formally defined alongside business impact analysis in Module 11. Here they function as design inputs.

📝 Think of it like…

Losing a document you were writing

Your machine crashes mid-document. Two separate questions follow. How much writing did you lose - ten minutes, or the whole afternoon? That is RPO, and it depends entirely on how often the file was saved.

Then: how long before you can write again - a reboot, or three days waiting for a replacement machine? That is RTO, and saving more often does nothing for it.

Confusing the two produces expensive mistakes in both directions: buying faster hardware when the problem was backup frequency, or backing up more often when the problem was having no spare machine.

Data Types and Classification

Data types

Objective 3.3 names several types, and they are categories of what the data is rather than how sensitive it is.

TypeDescriptionWhy it matters
RegulatedGoverned by law or industry rule - health, payment, personal dataHandling is legally mandated, not discretionary
Trade secretInformation deriving value from being secretLegal protection depends on you actually protecting it
Intellectual propertyPatents, designs, source code, creative workOften an organization's principal asset
Legal informationContracts, case material, privileged communicationDisclosure may forfeit privilege
Financial informationAccounts, forecasts, transactionsRegulatory and market-sensitive
Human-readableText a person can read directlyImmediately usable if exposed
Non-human-readableBinary, encoded, or machine formatsNot protection - obscurity is not security

Trade secret carries a distinctive property worth knowing. Unlike a patent, its legal protection depends on the holder having taken reasonable steps to keep it secret. An organization that fails to protect a trade secret may lose the legal status itself, which makes access control here a legal requirement rather than merely good practice.

And the human-readable distinction is a trap. Data being in a binary or encoded format is not protection - anyone with the right tool reads it trivially. Encoding is not encryption, and an exam option treating a non-human-readable format as a safeguard is wrong.

Classification

Classification labels data by sensitivity so that handling requirements follow automatically. Schemes vary between organizations, and CompTIA names these levels.

LevelMeaningTypical handling
PublicNo harm from disclosureNo restriction
PrivateInternal, personal, or non-publicInternal access only
SensitiveHarm if disclosedEncryption, controlled access
ConfidentialSignificant harm if disclosedStrong access control, logging
RestrictedSevere harm; often regulatedNeed-to-know, strict audit
CriticalEssential to operationsAvailability and integrity emphasis

Note that critical is doing something different from the others. The rest describe the harm of disclosure - a confidentiality scale. Critical describes the harm of losing the data, which is an availability judgement. A public price list can be critical: no harm if a competitor sees it, and the business stops if it disappears.

📚 Think of it like…

Sections of a library

The open shelves are public - anyone browses freely. The reference room requires a card. The rare books room requires an appointment and a supervised desk. The archive requires a documented reason and a countersignature.

Nobody decides these rules book by book. A book is assigned to a section, and the section's rules apply automatically. That is what classification buys you: label once, and handling follows without a fresh judgement for every file.

Where it breaks down: a book sits in one room. A spreadsheet is copied into email, a laptop, a backup, and a supplier's system within a week - which is why classification must travel with the data rather than describe where it lives.

Why classification often fails in practice

Classification schemes fail for predictable reasons, and the exam expects you to recognise them.

Too many levels. A scheme with seven tiers produces inconsistent labelling because nobody can reliably distinguish adjacent ones. Three or four well-understood levels outperform a precise scheme nobody applies correctly.

No owner. Someone must decide a given dataset's classification, and that someone should be the business owner rather than the security team, who do not know what the data is worth.

No consequences to the label. If confidential and private are handled identically in practice, the label is decoration. Classification only works when each level maps to specific, enforced handling requirements.

Who decides, and what a label must carry

Classification is a business decision implemented technically, and the exam expects you to know the split. The data owner is a senior business figure accountable for a dataset who determines its classification, because only they know what its loss would cost. The data custodian is the technical role implementing the resulting controls. Security advises; it does not decide.

These roles are defined formally in Module 12 alongside privacy obligations. What matters here is the principle: a security team choosing classifications for data it does not understand produces labels that are either uniformly high, which makes them meaningless, or arbitrary.

For a label to do any work it must travel with the data. A classification recorded only in a spreadsheet describes where the data was when someone last looked. Labels applied to the file itself - through metadata, headers, or document properties - survive copying, which is what allows automated controls such as data loss prevention to act on them.

Retention and disposal

Classification governs how long data is kept as well as how it is protected. Retention requirements come from two directions that frequently conflict: regulation mandating that certain records be held for a defined period, and privacy law requiring that personal data not be kept longer than necessary.

The security consequence is that data kept beyond its useful life is pure liability. It can still be breached, still triggers notification obligations, and still appears in discovery - while providing no operational value. Deleting data on schedule is a security control, and it is one of the few that reduces risk and cost simultaneously.

Disposal must match the classification. Restricted data on retired media requires sanitisation or physical destruction with certification, not simply deletion - a file marked deleted is generally still present until overwritten.

Protecting Data

Data states, briefly revisited

Module 2 covered the three states in detail. In summary: data at rest is protected by encryption and access control, data in transit by TLS or IPSec, and data in use is the difficult one, since a processor generally needs plaintext to compute on it.

What matters here is that a complete protection strategy must address all three. An organization encrypting disks and using TLS has covered two states and often believes it has covered the problem. Memory-scraping attacks target the third.

Data sovereignty and geolocation

Data sovereignty is the principle that data is subject to the laws of the country in which it is physically stored. This is not a technical constraint but a legal one, and it frequently dictates architecture.

The practical consequences are significant. An organization operating internationally may be legally required to keep certain records within a jurisdiction, which rules out providers or regions that cannot guarantee it. Cloud complicates this because data may be replicated across regions automatically for resilience - a feature that can create a compliance breach nobody chose.

Geographic restrictions, sometimes called geofencing, are the corresponding control: limiting where data may be stored or from where it may be accessed. Access-side restrictions are also a useful security control in their own right, since blocking authentication from countries where an organization has no staff removes a large volume of attack traffic.

🌐 Think of it like…

Property law follows the ground, not the owner

A building you own in another country obeys that country's planning rules, tax code, and rights of access - regardless of your nationality or where the deeds are kept. Your ownership does not exempt the building from local law.

Data works the same way. Records physically stored in a jurisdiction fall under its laws, including laws about government access, whoever owns them. This is why "where is it stored" is a compliance question before it is a technical one - and why automatic cross-region replication can create a legal problem without anyone making a decision.

Choosing a protection method

Objective 3.3 lists methods that Module 2 explained. The selection question is what matters here.

MethodOriginal recoverable?Choose it when
EncryptionYes, with the keyThe data must be usable again by authorised parties
HashingNoYou need to verify, never to read - passwords, integrity
TokenizationOnly by vault lookupSystems must reference the record without holding it
MaskingUsually no, in that viewPartial visibility suffices - support screens, test data
ObfuscationVariesRaising effort, never as a primary control
SegmentationN/ALimiting which systems can reach the data at all
Permission restrictionsN/ALimiting which people can reach it
Geographic restrictionsN/ASovereignty, or reducing access attack surface

The deciding question in almost every case is the second column. If the original value must be recoverable, hashing is eliminated immediately. If systems must recognise a repeat customer without storing card numbers, tokenization is the answer and masking is not, because masking discards rather than substitutes.

The last three rows are worth separating from the first five. Those are transformations applied to the data; these are restrictions on reaching it. A complete strategy uses both, which is defence in depth applied to data rather than to networks.

Selecting a method against a requirement

High Availability

Load balancing and clustering

Load balancing and clustering both improve availability, and the exam distinguishes them by what they do with capacity.

Load balancingClustering
PurposeDistribute work across serversEnsure the service survives a node failing
NodesAll active, sharing trafficActive-active or active-passive
Primary gainScalability, with availability as a by-productFailover and continuity
StateNodes often independentNodes coordinate and share state

The distinction the exam tests is active-active versus active-passive. In active-active every node carries traffic, so the capacity is used and a failure reduces available capacity. In active-passive a standby node does nothing until the primary fails, which wastes capacity and gives a cleaner, more predictable failover.

🛒 Think of it like…

Supermarket tills versus a relay team

Load balancing is the person directing shoppers to whichever till is shortest. Every till is open and serving; the point is throughput, and if one closes the others absorb the queue more slowly.

Clustering is a relay team. One runner carries the baton and another is ready to take over mid-race if they fall. The second runner is not making the team faster - they exist so the race does not end.

That is why the two answer different questions. Load balancing is primarily about handling more; clustering is primarily about surviving loss.

Platform diversity

Platform diversity means deliberately not standardising on a single vendor, operating system, or product for everything. A vulnerability or a vendor outage then affects part of the estate rather than all of it.

The trade-off is genuine and the exam expects both sides. Diversity buys resilience against a common-mode failure and costs standardisation: more expertise required, more patching processes, more configurations to get right. Homogeneous estates are cheaper to run well and fail completely when they fail.

🌾 Think of it like…

Planting one crop across every field

A single variety planted everywhere is efficient - one set of equipment, one schedule, one body of expertise. It is also why a single blight can take an entire harvest, as has happened historically more than once.

Mixed planting yields less per field and cannot fail all at once. The farmer is buying insurance against a correlated failure, paying for it in efficiency - which is exactly the platform diversity trade.

🏦 Think of it like…

Not keeping every account at one bank

Holding all your savings at a single bank is simple: one login, one statement, one set of rules to learn. It also means a failure, a freeze, or a dispute at that bank affects everything you have at once.

Spreading across two banks removes that single point of failure and costs you real effort - two sets of rules, two apps, two reconciliations, and twice the chance of getting one of them wrong through unfamiliarity.

Multi-cloud is the same bargain. Resilience against a correlated failure, paid for in complexity, which is itself a source of failure.

Multi-cloud

Multi-cloud extends the same reasoning to providers, using more than one so that a provider outage or a commercial dispute does not stop everything. It also reduces vendor lock-in and improves negotiating position.

The costs are substantial: each provider has different services, different identity models, and different security controls, so teams must maintain competence in all of them and reconcile policy across environments that do not agree on basic concepts. Multi-cloud adopted for resilience alone frequently produces more incidents than it prevents, because complexity is itself a source of failure.

Capacity planning

Objective 3.4 names three dimensions, and the first is the one organizations neglect.

DimensionThe questionFailure mode
PeopleDo we have enough trained staff?One person who knows how it works
TechnologyIs there enough compute, storage, bandwidth?Saturation under load
InfrastructureEnough power, cooling, space, connectivity?Physical limits reached

People capacity is the dimension exam scenarios use to catch you out. A perfectly engineered failover is worthless at three in the morning if the only person who understands it is unreachable. Cross-training, documentation, and defined on-call rotation are availability controls, not HR administration.

👤 Think of it like…

The one person who knows how the boiler works

A building has an unusual heating system that one caretaker understands completely. Everything runs beautifully for years. Then they retire, and nobody can restart it after a power cut - because the knowledge was never written down and nobody else was ever shown.

This is a capacity failure in the people dimension, and no amount of redundant hardware addresses it. It is why documentation and cross-training count as resilience measures.

Power

Two technologies with distinct roles, and the exam tests whether you know which covers which gap.

TechnologyCoversDuration
UPSInstantaneous failure; bridges the gapMinutes
GeneratorExtended outageHours to days, while fuel lasts

They are complementary rather than alternatives. A generator takes seconds to start and stabilise, during which everything would go down without a UPS. The UPS carries the load across that gap and then across a graceful shutdown if the generator fails to start.

The recurring real-world finding is that generators are installed, never load-tested, and fail when finally needed - because a generator that starts is not the same as a generator that carries the actual load. This is the same lesson as untested backups, in a different medium.

Backups and Recovery

The considerations named in the objective

ConsiderationWhat it decides
Onsite / offsiteWhether a site-level event destroys the backup too
FrequencyYour RPO - how much data a failure costs you
EncryptionWhether stolen backup media is a breach
SnapshotsPoint-in-time state, usually on the same platform
RecoveryWhether restoration has actually been tested
ReplicationContinuous copying to another location
JournalingA record of every change, allowing precise point-in-time recovery

Snapshots and replication are not backups

This distinction is heavily tested and frequently misunderstood in practice.

A snapshot captures state at a moment, usually stored on the same storage platform as the original. It is excellent for reverting a bad change quickly and useless if the platform itself fails or is encrypted by ransomware, because the snapshot is inside the blast radius.

Replication copies data continuously to another system. It gives excellent availability and faithfully replicates mistakes: deleted records and encrypted files are copied to the replica within seconds. Replication protects against a system failing, not against data being damaged.

A backup is a separate copy, held apart, from which a known-good state can be restored. The practical rule is 3-2-1: three copies, on two different media, with one offsite. Ransomware pushed many organizations to add a further requirement - at least one copy immutable or offline, since a backup reachable from a compromised network is a backup an attacker can encrypt.

📷 Think of it like…

A photograph in the same album

Photographing every page of an irreplaceable notebook and keeping the photographs in the same drawer protects you if you spill ink on a page. It protects you from nothing if the house burns down.

A snapshot is that photograph in the same drawer. A backup is a copy at your sister's house in another town. And replication is a machine that instantly reproduces every stroke you make in a second notebook - including the ink you just spilled.

📋 Think of it like…

How often you save the file

Backup frequency is not a technical preference dressed up as one. It is a direct statement of how much work the organization is willing to redo.

A team saving once a day has decided a day of work is an acceptable loss. If that is genuinely true, saving hourly is money spent buying something nobody needed. If it is not true, no amount of backup reliability rescues the decision - the frequency itself was the error.

Frequency and the recovery point

Backup frequency is not a technical preference; it is the direct expression of your RPO. Nightly backups mean that a failure at 16:00 loses everything since the previous night. If the business can tolerate that, nightly is correct and anything more frequent is wasted money. If it cannot, the frequency is wrong regardless of how reliable the backups are.

Continuous data protection and journaling exist for very low RPO requirements, recording each change so recovery can target a precise moment - immediately before a corrupting transaction, for example, rather than the previous midnight.

Recovery objectives against a real incident

Testing a restore for the first time

Full, incremental, and differential

Three approaches trade backup speed against restore speed, and the exam tests the restore side because that is what matters in an incident.

TypeCopiesBackup speedRestore requires
FullEverything, every timeSlowestThe one full backup
IncrementalChanges since the last backup of any kindFastestThe full, plus every increment since
DifferentialChanges since the last full backupMiddleThe full, plus one differential

The distinction that decides exam questions is what a restore needs. Incremental backups are quickest to take and slowest to restore, because every increment in the chain must be applied in order - and a single corrupt increment breaks everything after it. Differential backups grow larger each day but need only two pieces to restore.

An organization optimising purely for backup window length chooses incremental and discovers during an incident that restoration takes far longer than planned. That is an RTO failure caused by a backup design decision, which is why the two must be considered together.

The untested backup

The untested backup

The most common backup failure is not a missing backup. It is a backup that exists, reports success every night for two years, and cannot be restored - because the job was silently excluding a directory, the media degraded, the encryption key was lost, or nobody knows the procedure.

A backup is a hypothesis until a restore is performed. Regular restore testing is what converts it into a control, and the exam consistently rewards the answer that involves testing recovery rather than merely performing backups.

[EXAM] When a scenario describes backups that ran successfully but did not help, the intended answer is almost always that restoration was never tested - not that the backup software was inadequate.

Continuity, Sites, and Testing

Site resilience

If a facility becomes unusable, operations move somewhere else. The three site types trade cost against recovery time, and this is the most directly tested content in objective 3.4.

SiteStateRecovery timeCost
HotFully equipped, running, data currentMinutes to hoursHighest
WarmEquipped, needs data and configurationHours to daysModerate
ColdSpace, power, connectivity onlyDays to weeksLowest

The selection rule is simply your RTO. An organization needing to be operating within four hours cannot use a cold site whatever its budget, and one that can tolerate a fortnight is wasting money on a hot site. When a scenario gives you a recovery time requirement, it has given you the answer.

🚗 Think of it like…

Three ways to have a spare car

A hot site is a second car on the drive, fuelled, insured, and started weekly. You are moving in two minutes, and you are paying for a car you rarely drive.

A warm site is a car in the garage with a flat battery and no current insurance. It is genuinely yours and you will be driving by this afternoon.

A cold site is an empty parking space with your name on it. Cheap, real, and useless until you buy a car - which is the point. A cold site is a place to recover into, not a recovery.

🗝 Think of it like…

The spare key under the same doormat

Keeping a spare key protects you against losing yours. Keeping it under the doormat of the door it opens protects you against nothing that matters, because whatever reaches the door reaches the key.

A recovery site in the same industrial park is that doormat. It shares the power grid, the flood plain, and the road that closes - so the single event you are planning for takes both.

Geographic dispersion

An alternate site must be far enough away not to be affected by the same event. A backup facility in the same industrial park shares its power grid, its flood plain, and its transport links, so a single incident takes both.

The tension is that distance costs latency, which limits synchronous replication and complicates staff travel. The right distance is far enough to escape a shared regional event and close enough to remain workable - and "same city" is usually too close for anything but a building-level failure.

📚 Think of it like…

Knowing the ending but not the middle

A plan that describes the restored systems in detail and says nothing about the intervening two days is a story with a beginning and an ending and no middle. The organization knows precisely what normal looks like on either side of the gap.

Continuity is the middle. Who takes orders on paper, who is authorised to approve them, and how the backlog is reconciled when systems return. Disaster recovery restores the technology; continuity keeps the organization alive while it is gone.

Continuity of operations

A continuity of operations plan describes how the organization keeps functioning when normal capability is unavailable. It is broader than disaster recovery, which restores technology - continuity covers people, premises, suppliers, and manual workarounds.

The element most often missing is the manual workaround. If an ordering system is unavailable for two days, is there a paper process? Who is authorised to use it? How is the resulting backlog reconciled afterwards? Organizations plan the technology restoration thoroughly and leave the intervening days undefined.

Testing

A plan is a hypothesis until exercised. Objective 3.4 names four methods, increasing in realism and in risk.

MethodWhat happensDisruption
TabletopParticipants talk through a scenarioNone
SimulationA realistic scenario is enacted without touching productionLow
Parallel processingRecovery systems run alongside production; results comparedModerate
FailoverProduction is actually moved to the recovery siteHigh

Each finds different problems. A tabletop reveals that nobody knows who declares a disaster, or that the plan calls a supplier who is no longer contracted. Only a real failover reveals that the recovery site's certificate expired, its firewall rules were never updated, or its capacity is half what production needs.

Organizations tend to do tabletops repeatedly and never a full failover, because the tabletop is safe. This produces well-rehearsed confidence in a capability nobody has demonstrated - which is the untested backup problem at the scale of an entire site.

🔥 Think of it like…

Discussing the fire escape versus walking it

A meeting where staff describe the evacuation route establishes that people know the plan, and it is cheap and interrupts nothing. That is a tabletop.

Actually walking out finds the fire door blocked by pallets, the stairwell light out, and the assembly point now a building site. None of that appears in the discussion, because everyone describes the building as it was when the plan was written.

Where it breaks down: a fire drill costs ten minutes. A full failover risks a genuine outage, which is why organizations avoid it - and why the exam treats scheduling one as the mature answer rather than an obvious one.

[EXAM] Three discriminators to carry into the exam. RPO is set by backup frequency and RTO by recovery method - keep them apart. Snapshots and replication are not backups, because both stay inside the blast radius. And when backups exist but did not help, the answer is that restoration was never tested.
