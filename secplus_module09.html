Security+ · Module 9

Vulnerability Management, Monitoring, and Enterprise Controls

Finding vulnerabilities, converting severity into priority, choosing and validating a response, monitoring and alert tuning, and configuring the enterprise controls that enforce policy.

Progress: 0% complete

Overview

[OBJECTIVES] By the end of this module you will be able to compare the methods used to find vulnerabilities; interpret CVE and CVSS and explain why a base score is not a priority; select a response including patching, segmentation, or a documented exception; validate that remediation worked; describe monitoring tools and why alert tuning is security work; and configure the enterprise controls that enforce policy day to day.

This module spans three objectives and is the widest in the course. They belong together because they form one continuous loop: find what is wrong, decide what matters, fix it, confirm the fix, and watch for what the loop missed.

The management cycle

Vulnerability management is a cycle rather than a project, and the exam tests the stages by name.

StageQuestionCharacteristic failure
IdentifyWhat weaknesses exist?Scanning only part of the estate
AnalyseAre these findings real?Acting on false positives, missing false negatives
PrioritiseWhich matter here?Sorting by severity score alone
RemediateWhat is the response?Assuming patching is the only option
ValidateDid it actually work?Closing the ticket without rescanning
ReportWho needs to know?Technical output nobody can act on

The two stages organizations skip are prioritisation and validation. Skipping prioritisation produces a list of eleven thousand findings that nobody can act on. Skipping validation produces a closed ticket for a vulnerability that is still present.

🏥 Think of it like…

Triage in an emergency department

A department receiving forty patients at once does not treat them in arrival order, and it does not treat them in order of how dramatic the injury looks. It sorts by who will deteriorate soonest without intervention.

Vulnerability management works identically. A list sorted by severity score is arrival order dressed up as method - it ignores which systems are exposed, which are business-critical, and which vulnerabilities are being actively exploited today.

Finding Vulnerabilities

Vulnerability scanning

A scanner compares systems against a database of known vulnerabilities and reports what it finds. The distinction that matters most is how much access the scanner is given.

TypeAccessResult
CredentialedLogs in with an accountSees installed versions and configuration; far more accurate
Non-credentialedProbes from outsideSees what an unauthenticated attacker sees; more false positives

Both have a place. A credentialed scan gives the accurate internal picture used for remediation. A non-credentialed scan shows what an attacker without access would find, which is a different and also useful question.

Recall from Module 6 that active scanning can disturb fragile systems. On operational technology, passive discovery replaces active scanning for exactly that reason.

🔑 Think of it like…

An inspector with keys versus one looking through windows

An inspector who can enter the building reads the boiler's model plate, checks the fuse board, and reports precisely what is installed. An inspector limited to the pavement reports what is visible and guesses at the rest - and will describe a boarded window as a missing one.

That guessing is where false positives come from. Both reports are worth having: one tells you what is actually wrong, the other tells you what a passer-by can see.

Penetration testing is distinct from scanning in a way the exam tests. A scanner reports individual findings. A penetration test chains them - a low-severity information disclosure plus a medium-severity misconfiguration may together produce full compromise, and no scanner reports that combination because neither finding is severe alone.

Threat feeds and intelligence sources

SourceWhat it is
OSINTOpenly available information - advisories, research, public reporting
Proprietary / third-partyCommercial feeds from vendors with their own telemetry
Information-sharing organizationsSector groups exchanging intelligence between members
Dark web monitoringWatching criminal forums for stolen credentials or planned targeting

Their shared value is telling you what is being exploited now, which is what converts a severity score into a priority. A medium-severity vulnerability under mass exploitation this week outranks a critical one nobody has ever attacked.

🗳 Think of it like…

A neighbourhood watch bulletin

Knowing your back gate latch is weak is useful. Knowing that someone has been working along your street this week testing back gates changes what you do this evening.

The vulnerability did not change. What changed is the likelihood of it being tried, and that is precisely the contribution threat intelligence makes to prioritisation.

Responsible disclosure and bug bounties

A responsible disclosure programme gives researchers a lawful channel to report findings and commits the organization to respond within a stated period. A bug bounty adds payment.

The security value is economic rather than technical, and it is worth stating plainly: without a legitimate channel, a researcher who finds a flaw has the choice of reporting it to an organization that may respond with lawyers, publishing it, or selling it. A disclosure programme makes the first option safe and the bounty makes it attractive.

Analysis and Prioritisation

CVE and CVSS

StandardWhat it is
CVEA unique identifier for a specific vulnerability, so everyone refers to the same thing
CVSSA scoring system rating severity from 0.0 to 10.0

CVE is an index, not an assessment - it carries no severity, only identity. CVSS supplies the severity, banded as low, medium, high, and critical.

Why a CVSS base score is not a priority

This is the highest-yield idea in the module. A CVSS base score describes the vulnerability in the abstract: how hard it is to exploit, what privileges are needed, what it grants. It knows nothing about your environment.

Objective 4.3 names the factors that convert severity into priority.

FactorQuestion
Environmental variablesIs the affected system internet-facing or on an isolated segment?
Exposure factorHow much of the asset's value is lost if this is exploited?
Organizational impactIs this system business-critical or a test box?
Industry impactIs this vulnerability being exploited in your sector now?
Risk toleranceHow much residual risk is this organization prepared to hold?

The practical result is that a 9.8 on an isolated laboratory system with no sensitive data may sit below a 6.1 on an internet-facing payment service under active exploitation. An organization remediating strictly in score order is working hard in the wrong sequence.

⚠ Think of it like…

A hazard rating on a chemical

A label reading "highly flammable" describes the substance itself. It is accurate wherever the bottle sits, and it tells you nothing about whether you have a problem.

A sealed litre in a ventilated store with no ignition source is a different situation from an open bottle beside a gas hob in a nursery. The hazard rating is identical; the risk is not. CVSS is the hazard rating, and your environment supplies everything else.

Where it breaks down: a chemical sits where you put it. A vulnerable component may be installed on hosts nobody has inventoried, which is why the Module 8 asset record with software versions is what makes prioritisation possible at all.

Score order versus risk order

🚨 Think of it like…

A smoke alarm and a fire that never triggered it

An alarm sounding every time someone makes toast is a false positive. It is irritating, it wastes everyone's morning, and its real cost is that people start taking the battery out.

An alarm that stays silent during an actual fire is a false negative. Nobody is irritated, nobody investigates, and nothing whatsoever alerts you that the system failed.

That asymmetry is why false negatives get less attention despite mattering more. One produces noise you cannot ignore; the other produces silence indistinguishable from safety.

Practice: place the stage and the action

Confirmation: false positives and false negatives

Before anything is prioritised, findings must be confirmed. Module 5 introduced the four outcomes; here they apply to scan results.

A false positive is a reported vulnerability that is not actually present or not actually exploitable - a patch applied through a backported fix that leaves the version string unchanged is a classic cause. False positives waste effort and, worse, teach teams to distrust the scanner.

A false negative is a real vulnerability the scan did not report, and it is the dangerous one. Causes include hosts not scanned because they were offline or absent from the inventory, credentialed scans failing silently, and vulnerabilities the scanner's database does not yet cover.

The reason false positives receive more attention despite mattering less is that they are visible. A false negative produces no output at all, which is why coverage verification - confirming that everything you believe you scanned was actually scanned - is a distinct task from reading the results.

Response, Validation, and Reporting

Patching is one option among several

Objective 4.3 names the full response set, and the exam tests whether you know that remediation does not always mean applying a patch.

ResponseWhen it applies
PatchingA fix exists and can be applied - the default when available
SegmentationThe system cannot be patched; restrict what can reach it
Compensating controlsSubstitute protection where the primary control is unavailable
InsuranceTransfer financial consequence; reduces impact, not likelihood
Exceptions and exemptionsFormally accept the risk, with an owner and a review date

Insurance is worth pausing on because students misread it. Cyber insurance transfers some financial consequence of an incident. It does not reduce the chance of the incident, does not restore data, and does not satisfy a regulator. It belongs in the list as a risk treatment rather than as a technical remedy.

Exceptions and exemptions done properly

Sometimes a vulnerability will not be remediated - the fix breaks a critical application, the vendor has no patch, the cost exceeds the risk. That is a legitimate outcome, and an exception is only legitimate when documented.

A defensible exception records what the vulnerability is, why it will not be remediated, what compensating controls are in place, who accepted the risk by name, and when the decision will be reviewed. Without these it is not an exception; it is an unremediated vulnerability with a story attached.

The named acceptor matters most. Risk acceptance is a business decision, so it belongs to someone with authority to make it - not to the security analyst who happened to close the ticket.

📋 Think of it like…

A signed variance versus an ignored rule

A builder who cannot meet a requirement can apply for a variance: the deviation is described, the mitigation is specified, an official signs it, and it is reviewed at the next inspection. The building is lawful.

A builder who simply ignores the requirement has produced the same physical outcome and none of the protection. When something goes wrong, one of them has a document naming who decided and why, and the other has a problem nobody will admit to authorising.

🔧 Think of it like…

Signing off the repair without checking

A landlord sends a plumber, receives an invoice, and marks the leak resolved. Six weeks later the ceiling comes down. The plumber attended, and something about the job did not take.

Marking a vulnerability closed when the patch was deployed is the same move. Deployment is the invoice; validation is going back into the room and looking at the pipe.

Validation

Remediation is a claim until it is verified. Objective 4.3 names three methods.

MethodWhat it confirms
RescanningThe finding no longer appears
AuditThe process was followed, not just this instance fixed
VerificationManual confirmation, often for findings scanners handle poorly

The recurring failure is closing a ticket when a patch is deployed rather than when it is confirmed applied. Deployment and application are different events, as Module 4's patch management process noted - a patch that failed silently on forty machines produces a report saying the vulnerability is closed while forty systems remain exposed.

📈 Think of it like…

A doctor handing over the raw bloodwork

A patient handed twelve pages of reference ranges and values has been given everything and told nothing. The information is complete, accurate, and useless to the person who has to make a decision.

A four-thousand-row scanner export sent to a board is the same document. The technical team genuinely needs those rows. The board needs to know whether the backlog is growing and how long critical findings stay open - and if nobody can see what the programme achieved, it loses its funding.

Measuring whether the programme works

Reporting needs measures rather than counts, and a handful recur across mature programmes.

MeasureWhat it reveals
Mean time to remediateHow long a finding stays open, usually banded by severity
Backlog trendWhether the team is gaining or losing ground overall
Scan coverageWhat proportion of known assets was actually assessed
Findings past dueWhere agreed remediation timescales are being missed
Open exceptionsHow much risk has been formally accepted, and by whom

Scan coverage is the one most often absent and the one that undermines everything else. A report stating that 98% of findings are remediated means very little if only 60% of assets were scanned - the remaining 40% contributes no findings at all, which reads identically to being clean. This is the false negative problem expressed as a metric.

Reporting

Reporting

Reporting is listed as a stage because output that nobody can act on has not completed the cycle. The distinction the exam draws is audience.

Technical teams need specifics: affected hosts, the CVE, the fix. An executive audience needs trend and exposure - is the backlog growing or shrinking, how long do critical findings stay open, which business services carry the most risk. A four-thousand-row scanner export sent upward communicates nothing, and one of the more common reasons vulnerability programmes lose funding is that nobody could see what they achieved.

Alerting and Monitoring

What is monitored, and the activities involved

Objective 4.4 divides monitoring across systems, applications, and infrastructure, and names the activities that make it work.

ActivityPurpose
Log aggregationBring records from many sources into one place so they can be correlated
AlertingSurface events matching defined conditions
ScanningContinuous discovery of vulnerabilities and configuration issues
ReportingCommunicate posture and trend
ArchivingRetain records long enough to investigate what began months ago
Alert responseTriage, then remediate or validate - including quarantine
Alert tuningAdjust rules so alerts remain trustworthy

Archiving deserves emphasis because retention is usually set by cost. The dwell times in Module 3 mean an intrusion may have begun long before detection, and logs retained for thirty days cannot describe an incident that started in month four.

📹 Think of it like…

Many cameras, one control room

A hundred cameras each with its own screen in its own cupboard is not a surveillance system - it is a hundred recordings nobody watches. Bringing the feeds into one room lets an operator notice that the person who entered the loading bay at 02:14 appeared at the server room door at 02:19.

That correlation is the whole point of a SIEM. Neither event was remarkable alone, and the connection between them existed only once both were in the same place.

Alert tuning is security work

Module 5 introduced alert fatigue. Here it is the operational task named in the objective, and it deserves to be understood as engineering rather than housekeeping.

A rule generating two hundred alerts a day, of which one is real, will be dismissed quickly by analysts and eventually ignored entirely. The detection capability then exists on paper and nowhere else. Tuning - narrowing conditions, suppressing known-benign patterns, adjusting thresholds to the environment's baseline - is what keeps a rule trustworthy.

The trade-off is real: tuning too aggressively creates false negatives. The judgement is contextual, which is why a rule appropriate on twelve sensitive servers may be unusable across ten thousand endpoints.

Quarantine and alert response

Objective 4.4 names quarantine as part of alert response, and it covers several distinct actions with a shared purpose: stop the spread without destroying what you need to investigate.

ActionWhat it achieves
Isolate a host from the networkHalts lateral movement and exfiltration while the system stays running
Quarantine a fileRemoves it from execution without deleting the sample
Hold an email in a quarantine queuePrevents delivery pending analyst review
Disable an accountStops credential use without deleting audit history

Every one of these deliberately stops short of destruction, and the reason recurs in incident response: powering off a compromised host loses memory contents, and deleting a malicious file loses the sample needed to determine what it did and whether it appears elsewhere. Containment and eradication are separate steps, and Module 11 covers why the order matters.

Vulnerability scannersThe identification tooling from earlier in this module

NetFlow is frequently misunderstood. It records the metadata of conversations rather than their content, which makes it lightweight enough to keep for long periods and useful even when traffic is encrypted. You cannot read what was said, and you can see that a workstation sent 41 GB to an unfamiliar address overnight - which is the exfiltration indicator from Module 5.

📞 Think of it like…

An itemised phone bill

A phone bill does not record what was said. It records who called whom, when, and for how long - and that is often enough to establish that something is wrong. A number never dialled before, called at three in the morning, for ninety minutes, is a finding without a single word of the conversation.

NetFlow is that bill. It is also why encryption does not blind network monitoring the way people assume: the content is unreadable and the pattern is not.

Enterprise Security Controls

Objective 4.5 is about modifying enterprise capabilities. Module 6 covered where these devices sit; this section covers what you configure on them.

Firewalls and IDS/IPS in operation

Firewall work is rule management: access control lists specifying source, destination, port, and action, applied in order, ending in an implicit deny. Two operational points recur in exam questions. Rule order matters, because a permissive rule earlier in the list makes a restrictive rule below it unreachable. And rules accumulate - the undocumented rule from Module 1's lab exists in most production firewalls, which is why periodic rule review is a control.

For IDS and IPS the objective names signatures and trends. Signature detection matches known patterns and is precise and blind to anything new. Trend or anomaly detection flags deviation from baseline and catches novel behaviour at the cost of more false positives. This is the deny-list-versus-behaviour trade from Module 5 in another form.

📪 Think of it like…

A blocked address versus a blocked street name

DNS filtering refuses to look up a name. It is like a post office declining to deliver to a known fraudulent address - efficient, because the letter never leaves, and broad, because it applies whatever the letter contains.

Its limit follows from the same mechanism. Someone who writes out the map coordinates instead of the street name has bypassed the check entirely, which is exactly what an attacker connecting to a direct address does.

Web and DNS filtering

ApproachHow it works
Centralised proxyTraffic routed through an inspection point on the network
Agent-basedSoftware on the endpoint filters wherever the device is
URL scanningChecks the specific address requested
Content categorisationClassifies sites into categories that policy permits or blocks
ReputationScores destinations by observed behaviour and age
Block rulesExplicit allow or deny for named destinations

Agent-based filtering has largely displaced the centralised proxy for one reason: a proxy only inspects traffic that passes through it, and remote workers do not. This is the same argument that produced SASE in Module 6.

DNS filtering blocks resolution of known-malicious domains, which is efficient because it stops the connection before it is attempted and covers every protocol at once rather than only web traffic. Its limit is that it operates on names, so an attacker using a direct address bypasses it entirely.

Email security: SPF, DKIM, and DMARC

These three are heavily tested and easily confused. They answer different questions and are designed to work together.

MechanismQuestion it answers
SPFIs this sending server authorised to send for this domain?
DKIMWas this message signed by the domain, and is it unmodified?
DMARCWhat should a receiver do when SPF or DKIM fails, and where are reports sent?

SPF publishes which servers may send for your domain. DKIM adds a signature the receiver verifies against a published key - the Module 2 digital signature applied to email. DMARC ties them together by declaring a policy (none, quarantine, or reject) and providing a reporting address, which is what lets an organization discover who is spoofing its domain.

Their shared limit is worth restating from Module 3: these mechanisms authenticate the domain. An attacker sending from a lookalike domain they genuinely control can publish perfectly valid records and pass all three checks, which is why out-of-band verification remains the control for payment fraud.

✉ Think of it like…

Three checks on a letter

SPF is checking that the letter was posted from a sorting office the company actually uses. DKIM is the wax seal proving the company sealed it and nobody opened it in transit. DMARC is the standing instruction to the mailroom saying what to do with a letter failing either check - bin it, hold it, or let it through - and to send a weekly summary of what was rejected.

Where it breaks down: all three verify the sender is who the envelope claims. None of them notices that the company name on the envelope is spelled with one letter different from the real one.

Evaluating an inbound message

🪤 Think of it like…

A tripwire on a door that should never open

Some doors in a building are genuinely never used - a sealed service hatch, a disused loading entrance. You do not need to watch them, judge who is passing through, or decide whether the person had a reason. You only need to know if they move at all.

File integrity monitoring is that tripwire. System binaries do not change during normal operation, so the alert requires no judgement about intent - the movement itself is the finding.

The remaining controls

User behaviour analytics is the one worth understanding rather than memorising. It builds a per-user baseline and flags deviation from it - which is what catches an insider whose activity is entirely permitted but completely unlike them, and stolen credentials being used by someone who behaves nothing like the account's owner.

Group Policy and SELinux are the enforcement mechanisms behind Module 8's baselines. A baseline is a document; these are how it becomes the actual configuration of ten thousand machines.

🤹 Think of it like…

The regular who is behaving oddly

A bartender who has served the same customer weekly for years notices immediately when they arrive at a different time, order something they never drink, and pay with a card instead of cash. Nothing observed is against any rule.

That is user behaviour analytics. It does not detect prohibited actions - other controls do that. It detects permitted actions performed by someone who does not behave like the person whose account it is.
