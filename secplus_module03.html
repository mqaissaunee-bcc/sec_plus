Threat Actors and Attack Surfaces

Who attacks organizations and why, the routes attacks travel, the social engineering family, and how attack surface is measured and reduced.

Progress: 0% complete

Overview

[OBJECTIVES] By the end of this module you will be able to compare the six threat actor types by resources, sophistication, and position; explain the motivations that drive each; identify the vectors through which attacks arrive; recognise the social engineering family and what distinguishes its members; and describe what an attack surface is and how it is reduced.

Domain 2 is the second-largest on the exam at 22%, and this module opens it. The material is less technical than what follows, which leads students to skim it. That is a mistake for a specific reason: nearly every scenario question in Domain 2 begins by describing an adversary, and your first job is to work out who you are dealing with. Get the actor wrong and the rest of the reasoning follows the wrong path.

Consider two scenarios that look similar. In the first, an organization finds credentials being sold on a criminal forum weeks after a breach. In the second, an organization finds an intruder who has been present for fourteen months, took nothing of monetary value, and specifically accessed engineering documents. Both are breaches. But the first is organized crime pursuing financial gain, and the second is a nation-state conducting espionage - and almost every question that follows, from likely technique to appropriate response, diverges from that point.

Threat, vulnerability, and risk

These three words are used loosely in conversation and precisely on the exam. Keep them separate.

TermDefinitionExample
ThreatSomething with the potential to cause harmA ransomware group
VulnerabilityA weakness that could be exploitedAn unpatched server
RiskThe likelihood and impact of a threat exploiting a vulnerabilityProbability of that server being encrypted, and what it would cost

A threat with no matching vulnerability produces little risk, and a vulnerability nobody is interested in exploiting produces little risk either. Risk is where the two intersect - which is why threat actor analysis matters operationally and not just academically. Knowing who is likely to target you determines which vulnerabilities actually matter.

🏠 Think of it like…

A window on the ground floor

The unlocked window is the vulnerability. Burglars operating in your area are the threat. The risk is the chance that one of them tries your window, multiplied by what they would take.

Notice how the pieces interact. The same unlocked window on a remote island with no burglars carries almost no risk. The same burglars outside a house with no windows carry almost no risk either. Security work is mostly about finding where the two overlap.

[EXAM] Questions frequently give you a scenario and ask which actor is most likely responsible. The answer is driven by the combination of what was taken, how long they were present, and how sophisticated the method was - not by any single detail.

Threat Actors

The three attributes that define an actor

CompTIA describes actors along three attributes, and the exam uses them as the distinguishing features between otherwise similar options.

AttributeQuestion it answers
Internal or externalDo they already have legitimate access?
Resources and fundingCan they sustain a long campaign, buy exploits, employ staff?
Level of sophisticationDo they write their own tools or download someone else's?

Sophistication and resources usually travel together, but not always, and the exception matters. An insider may have very low sophistication and no funding whatsoever, yet cause enormous damage because their position substitutes for both. They do not need to break in.

Nation-state actors

Government-sponsored, effectively unlimited in funding, and the most sophisticated adversary in the catalogue. They employ full-time staff, develop custom tooling, and can afford to acquire or discover zero-day vulnerabilities. Crucially, they are patient: a nation-state campaign may run for years.

This is where the term advanced persistent threat comes from, and each word is doing work. Advanced refers to capability. Persistent means the goal is long-term access rather than a quick score. Threat signals a human adversary adapting to your defences rather than automated malware running a script.

Typical targets are government systems, defence contractors, critical infrastructure, and any organization holding intellectual property of strategic value. Typical motivations are espionage, war, and disruption - rarely direct financial gain.

👤 Think of it like…

A houseguest who never leaves

A burglar is in and out in ten minutes and you know immediately, because the television is gone. Now imagine someone who moves into your spare room, takes nothing you would notice, learns your routine, copies your documents as you file them, and stays for a year.

You would not detect that by counting your possessions, because nothing is missing. That is what persistent means, and it is why nation-state intrusions are typically discovered by noticing unusual behaviour rather than by noticing loss.

Where it breaks down: a houseguest is one person in one house. A nation-state campaign may run against hundreds of organizations at once, and may compromise your supplier specifically to reach you.

Unskilled attackers

Formerly called script kiddies, these are individuals using tools built by others without understanding how they work. Sophistication is low and resources are minimal. Motivations are typically curiosity, reputation among peers, or simple disruption.

Low sophistication does not mean low impact. Prebuilt tooling is genuinely powerful, and booter or stresser services will run a denial of service attack for a small fee. An unskilled attacker with a rented botnet can take an unprepared organization offline. What they cannot do is adapt when the obvious approach fails, which is the practical distinction.

🔧 Think of it like…

A power tool without the training

Someone who buys a professional lock-picking gun online can open a cheap lock on their first attempt. The tool encodes the expertise, and the result on that lock is the same as a professional's.

But the moment they meet a lock the tool does not handle, they stop. They cannot diagnose why it failed or improvise, because the knowledge was never theirs. That is precisely the boundary between an unskilled attacker and a sophisticated one - not what they can do on a good day, but what they do when the first approach fails.

Hacktivists

Hacktivists are driven by political or philosophical belief rather than profit. Sophistication varies widely, from loosely coordinated volunteers to genuinely skilled operators. Their defining characteristic is that publicity is part of the objective. A criminal wants the breach unnoticed for as long as possible; a hacktivist frequently announces it.

Common techniques are website defacement, denial of service against a symbolic target, and doxing - publishing private information to embarrass or endanger. Targets are chosen for what they represent, which means an organization's risk from hacktivism depends on its public profile rather than its assets.

Insider threats

An insider is anyone with legitimate access: an employee, contractor, or partner. They bypass the perimeter entirely because they were invited through it, and they know where valuable data is and which controls exist.

The exam expects you to distinguish two kinds. A malicious insider acts deliberately, motivated by revenge after a disciplinary action, financial gain from selling data, or ideology. An unintentional insider causes harm through error - misconfiguring a storage bucket, falling for a phish, or emailing a spreadsheet to the wrong recipient. Unintentional insiders cause far more incidents; malicious insiders cause more damage per incident.

[EXAM] Insider scenarios often mention a recent negative employment event - a demotion, a poor review, an announced redundancy. That detail is rarely incidental. It is signalling motive.

🔑 Think of it like…

The locksmith who fitted your locks

Every external control you own assumes the threat is outside. The locksmith was never outside. They know which door is weakest, which window was never rewired into the alarm, and when you go on holiday - because you told them.

No amount of strengthening the perimeter addresses this. The answers are different in kind: least privilege so their access is bounded, separation of duties so no single person can complete a harmful action alone, and monitoring so unusual behaviour is visible.

Organized crime

Organized crime operates as a business, and the exam expects you to recognise that framing. These groups are well funded, moderately to highly sophisticated, and motivated almost entirely by money. They employ developers, run help desks for ransomware victims, and offer ransomware-as-a-service to affiliates who lack the skill to build their own.

Because the motive is financial, targeting is largely opportunistic and driven by return on investment. They pursue whoever is likely to pay, which is why healthcare, local government, and mid-sized businesses are disproportionately affected - not because they hold the most valuable data, but because downtime is intolerable and defences are often thinner than in large enterprises.

Shadow IT

Shadow IT is technology used within an organization without approval or the knowledge of the IT function: an unsanctioned cloud storage account, a departmental SaaS subscription bought on a corporate card, a personal device holding company data.

The distinction the exam draws is that shadow IT is almost never malicious. It arises when official systems are too slow or too restrictive and staff route around them to do their jobs. That makes it a symptom as much as a cause. It still creates real exposure, because it is unmonitored, unpatched, and invisible to the security team - assets nobody knows about cannot be protected.

Motivations

Motivation is what an attacker wants, and it is the most useful single clue in a scenario question. It shapes what they take, how long they stay, and whether they want you to know.

The motivations named in the objective

🏢 Think of it like…

Three break-ins at the same shop

In the first, the till is emptied and the thief is gone in four minutes. In the second, nothing is missing at all, but the safe holding the product designs was opened and photographed, and it happened eleven months ago. In the third, nothing is stolen and the windows are smashed with a slogan painted across the shutter.

Same premises, three entirely different adversaries. The evidence that identifies them is not how they got in - it is what they wanted. Financial gain, espionage, and political statement leave visibly different scenes.

Reading motivation from evidence

Scenario questions describe an incident and expect you to infer. These signals are the ones the exam uses most.

Evidence in the scenarioPoints toward
Long dwell time, nothing obviously stolenEspionage, nation-state
Rapid encryption and a ransom demandFinancial gain, organized crime
Public announcement, defacement, a manifestoPolitical belief, hacktivist
Targeted at engineering or research data specificallyEspionage or IP theft
Damage timed to a departure or disciplinary actionRevenge, insider
Widely available tooling, no attempt to hideUnskilled attacker
Attack on utilities during a geopolitical conflictWar, nation-state

The strongest single discriminator is dwell time. A criminal wants to monetise quickly and leaves. A spy wants to remain. When a scenario mentions how long an intruder was present, that number is almost always the intended clue.

Why motivation changes the response

This is not an academic exercise. If an intrusion is criminal and financially motivated, the priority is containment, restoration, and preventing payment pressure from driving bad decisions. If it is espionage, containment alone is inadequate: an actor who has been present for a year has likely established multiple footholds, and rebuilding systems without a thorough hunt simply invites them back through a door you never found.

If the actor is an insider, the response involves human resources and legal counsel from the first hour, and evidence handling matters far more because the matter may end in employment tribunal or prosecution.

Threat Vectors

A threat vector is the route an attack takes to reach you. Objective 2.2 lists them explicitly, and the exam tests whether you can name the vector from a description of how something arrived.

🚪 Think of it like…

Every opening in a building

A building has a front door, a loading bay, fire exits, windows, ventilation ducts, and a mail slot. Each is a route inside. Some are watched, some are locked, and some nobody has thought about in years.

An attacker does not care which one you consider the main entrance. They will use whichever is least defended, which is why counting your openings honestly matters more than reinforcing the one you already watch.

Message-based vectors

Email remains the dominant delivery route for attacks, joined by SMS and instant messaging. The message may carry a malicious attachment, a link to a credential-harvesting page, or simply an instruction to a human - a request to change bank details, which needs no malware at all.

Related is the image-based vector, where malicious content is embedded in image files, and the file-based vector, covering documents with malicious macros, archives, and installers.

Voice calls

Telephone-based attacks exploit the immediacy and social pressure of a live conversation. A caller impersonating IT support or a senior executive can extract credentials or push someone into an action they would question in writing. Attackers increasingly combine channels - an email that primes the target, followed by a call that closes.

Removable devices

A USB drive bypasses every network control by physically crossing the perimeter. The classic technique is leaving infected drives where staff will find them, relying on curiosity. Some devices are worse than they appear: a malicious USB may present itself as a keyboard and type commands the moment it is inserted, so the user need not open anything at all.

💽 Think of it like…

A dropped wallet on the pavement

Most people who find a wallet will open it - not to steal, but to find out whose it is. The impulse is helpful rather than careless.

USB drops exploit exactly that impulse. The label says "Payroll Q3" or "Redundancies", and the person who plugs it in is often trying to return it to the right owner. This is why the countermeasure is technical (blocking removable media) rather than an instruction to be more suspicious - the behaviour being exploited is a good one.

Vulnerable and unsupported software

Software with known vulnerabilities is a standing invitation, and the objective distinguishes client-based from agentless approaches to managing it. Client-based requires software installed on each endpoint, giving deep visibility but requiring deployment and maintenance. Agentless requires no installation but sees less.

Unsupported systems and applications are a distinct and worse case. A vulnerable system can be patched; an end-of-life system cannot, because no patch will ever be written. This is the compensating control scenario from Module 1 in its most common real form.

Unsecure networks

The objective names wireless, wired, and Bluetooth. Open or weakly encrypted wireless allows interception; unsecured wired ports in publicly accessible areas allow direct network access; and Bluetooth carries its own family of attacks against pairing and discovery.

Public wireless deserves particular attention because an attacker can operate a network that looks entirely legitimate - a rogue access point named identically to a coffee shop's - and see everything not otherwise encrypted.

Open service ports and default credentials

Every listening service is a potential entry point, and services are routinely exposed unintentionally through misconfiguration. Internet-wide scanning is continuous and automated, so a newly exposed service is typically discovered within minutes, not weeks.

Default credentials are the simplest vector in the entire list and remain among the most effective. Default usernames and passwords for essentially every device are published and searchable. A device deployed without changing them is not so much vulnerable as openly available.

Supply chain

Rather than attacking a well-defended target directly, an attacker compromises something the target already trusts - a software vendor, a managed service provider, or a hardware supplier. The malicious code then arrives through a legitimate, signed, expected channel.

The objective names three routes: managed service providers, who hold privileged access to many client networks at once; vendors, whose software updates are trusted implicitly; and suppliers, including hardware that can be tampered with before delivery.

Supply chain attacks are efficient in a way that makes them structurally attractive: compromising one MSP may deliver access to hundreds of downstream organizations, each of which had no realistic opportunity to detect the compromise upstream.

🚚 Think of it like…

Contaminating the ingredient, not the meal

Poisoning one restaurant means getting past that restaurant's kitchen staff. Contaminating a spice sold to four hundred restaurants means getting past one supplier's quality control, and every kitchen then serves the dish willingly - because the ingredient arrived through the proper channel with the right paperwork.

Where it breaks down: a restaurant can inspect an ingredient on arrival. Most organizations cannot meaningfully inspect a signed software update, which is why supply chain defence relies on vendor assessment and monitoring for unusual behaviour after installation rather than on inspection.

Social Engineering

Social engineering attacks the person rather than the system. It is listed as a human vector in objective 2.2 and appears throughout the exam, because it remains the most common initial access method in real breaches. No technical control is bypassed when a legitimate user is persuaded to open the door.

The principles being exploited

PrincipleHow it is used
AuthorityThe message appears to come from an executive or the police
UrgencyAct now or the account closes, the payment fails, the deal collapses
ScarcityLimited slots, limited time, act before it is gone
FamiliarityThe sender seems to know you or your colleagues
TrustBuilt over several harmless exchanges before the request
IntimidationThreats of consequences for non-compliance
Social proofEveryone else has already done this

Urgency combined with authority is the most common pairing by a wide margin, because together they suppress the verification step. A message from the chief executive demanding action in the next ten minutes is engineered specifically to prevent the recipient from picking up a phone.

The phishing family

TermChannelTargeting
PhishingEmailMass, untargeted
Spear phishingEmailSpecific individual or small group, researched
WhalingEmailSenior executives specifically
VishingVoice callEither
SmishingSMSEither

Two axes only: channel and how targeted it is. Vishing is voice, smishing is SMS, spear phishing is researched, whaling is aimed at the top. Exam questions distinguish these by naming the channel or describing the level of personalisation, and options will offer several members of the family at once.

🎣 Think of it like…

A net versus a spear

Phishing casts a net. Ten thousand identical letters go out and a handful of people respond; the sender never knew any of their names.

Spear phishing throws a spear at one fish. The message references your actual project, names your actual manager, and arrives the week your actual renewal is due - because the attacker read your website, your LinkedIn, and your press releases first. Whaling is the same spear aimed at the largest fish in the water, where a single success is worth the research.

Business email compromise

Business email compromise deserves separate attention because it involves no malware at all, which makes it invisible to controls that look for malicious files. An attacker either compromises or convincingly impersonates a legitimate business account and uses it to request a payment or a change of bank details.

Because the request arrives from a real or realistic address, uses the organization's own language, and often references a genuine transaction, it defeats both technical filtering and casual scepticism. The standard countermeasure is procedural rather than technical: out-of-band verification, where any change to payment details is confirmed by phoning a number already on file rather than one supplied in the message.

Impersonation, pretexting, and watering holes

Impersonation is claiming to be someone else - IT support, a delivery driver, an auditor. Pretexting is the fabricated backstory that makes the request plausible; the two usually appear together, with the pretext supplying the reason the impersonated person would be asking.

A watering hole attack compromises a third-party site the intended victims are known to visit - an industry forum, a local news site, a supplier's portal - and waits. The attacker never contacts the target directly, which is what makes it difficult to detect from the target's perspective.

🦇 Think of it like…

Waiting at the waterhole

A predator that chases prey across the plain expends enormous energy and is visible the whole time. A predator that waits where the herd must eventually drink expends almost nothing and is not visible until it is too late.

That is the watering hole attack exactly. Rather than attacking a hardened target, compromise the industry forum its engineers read every morning and let them come to you.

Brand impersonation, typosquatting, and misinformation

Brand impersonation replicates a trusted company's visual identity to make a fraudulent message credible. Typosquatting registers domains that are near-misses for real ones - a transposed letter, a different suffix - to catch mistyped addresses or to make a link survive a glance.

The objective also names misinformation and disinformation. The distinction is intent: misinformation is false information spread without knowing it is false, while disinformation is deliberately created and spread to deceive. Disinformation campaigns are typically a nation-state technique aimed at influence rather than access.

🏪 Think of it like…

A shop that opens next door with almost your name

A customer looking for Harrison & Co walks into Harisson & Co, which has the same signage, the same colours, and a similar shopfront. They will hand over their money without ever registering the missing letter, because they were not reading the sign - they were recognising it.

Typosquatting works because humans pattern-match rather than parse. That is also why hovering over a link before clicking is genuinely effective advice: it converts recognition into reading.

Attack Surface and Countermeasures

What an attack surface is

An organization's attack surface is the total set of points where an attacker could attempt entry. It includes every internet-facing service, every endpoint, every employee who can be contacted, every third party with access, and every system nobody remembers owning.

The important property is that attack surface grows on its own. Every new SaaS subscription, every new integration, every new hire expands it. Reduction is deliberate work; expansion is the default.

🏭 Think of it like…

Bricking up the doors you no longer use

An old factory converted over decades has doors that once served loading bays, offices, and a canteen that closed in 1994. Each is a maintenance burden and a way in, and nobody has checked most of them in years.

Attack surface reduction is walking the building, finding every opening, and bricking up the ones that serve no current purpose. The doors that remain get proper locks and are actually watched. Notice the prerequisite: you cannot brick up a door you do not know exists, which is why asset inventory precedes everything else.

Reducing the surface

MeasureWhat it removes
Asset inventoryUnknown systems - the prerequisite for everything else
DecommissioningSystems still running with no owner or purpose
Closing unused ports and servicesListening services nobody needs
Changing default credentialsThe simplest and most reliable vector
Patching and upgradingKnown exploitable vulnerabilities
Network segmentationThe ability to move laterally after entry
Least privilegeThe value of any single compromised account
Blocking removable mediaThe physical bypass of network controls
Sanctioned alternatives to shadow ITThe incentive to route around IT

Note the last row. Shadow IT is not solved by prohibition, because it exists as a response to friction. Blocking a file-sharing service without supplying a workable alternative moves the behaviour somewhere less visible rather than stopping it.

Countering social engineering

Because social engineering targets people, the countermeasures are a mix of human and technical, and the exam expects both.

CountermeasureWhat it addresses
Security awareness trainingRecognition of common patterns
Simulated phishing campaignsMeasured behaviour rather than claimed knowledge
Out-of-band verificationBusiness email compromise and payment fraud
A blameless reporting channelDelay between click and disclosure
Email authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC)Spoofed sender domains
Multifactor authenticationValue of harvested credentials
Link and attachment filteringDelivery of malicious content

The reporting channel is more important than its position on that list suggests. The damage from a phish is largely a function of how long it takes to be reported, and an employee who fears punishment will delay or stay silent. A culture that thanks people for reporting their own mistakes detects incidents in minutes rather than weeks - which is why blame-free reporting is a security control and not merely a nicety.

[EXAM] When a scenario describes payment fraud or a change to bank details, the expected answer is almost always out-of-band verification using contact details already held - not better filtering, and not more training.
