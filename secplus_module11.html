Governance and Risk Management

Policies, standards, procedures and guidelines, the roles held over systems and data, the risk management process, quantitative risk analysis, and the strategies for responding to risk.

Progress: 0% complete

Overview

[OBJECTIVES] By the end of this module you will be able to distinguish policies, standards, procedures, and guidelines and say which is which from a description; identify governance structures and the roles held over systems and data; run the risk management process from identification to reporting; perform quantitative risk analysis using SLE, ARO, and ALE; and select a risk management strategy and defend it.

Domain 5 is Security Program Management and Oversight at 20% - the second-largest domain, and the least technical. That combination catches students out. The material reads easily, which produces false confidence, and the questions turn on precise distinctions between terms that sound interchangeable in ordinary speech.

Three pairs cause most of the losses: policy versus standard, risk appetite versus risk tolerance, and data controller versus data processor. Each pair is genuinely different, each is examinable, and none of the differences is intuitive.

Why governance exists at all

Every technical control in the previous ten modules answers a decision somebody made. Governance is where those decisions are made, recorded, and given an owner - so that the control exists because the organization chose it rather than because an engineer had a preference on a Tuesday.

The practical test of governance is whether an organization can answer three questions about any control: who decided this, on what basis, and who reviews it. An organization that cannot answer them has controls rather than a security programme.

🏛 Think of it like…

The difference between rules and habits

A kitchen where everyone happens to wash their hands is not the same as a kitchen with a hygiene policy. Both may be spotless today. Only one stays spotless when the experienced staff leave, when a new manager arrives with different instincts, or when an inspector asks why.

Governance converts habits into commitments that survive the people who formed them. That is why Domain 5 exists alongside the technical domains rather than instead of them.

Governance Documents and Structures

The document hierarchy

Four document types, in descending order of abstraction. The exam gives you a description and expects you to name the type.

TypeWhat it doesMandatory?Example
PolicyStates intent and assigns responsibilityYes"Company data must be protected against unauthorised disclosure"
StandardSpecifies the measurable requirementYes"Passwords must be at least 14 characters"
ProcedureGives the step-by-step methodYes"To reset a password: open the console, select..."
GuidelineOffers recommended practiceNo"Consider using a passphrase of unrelated words"

Two tests resolve almost every question here. Is it mandatory? If optional, it is a guideline, whatever it is called. Is it measurable? A policy states what must be true; a standard states the specific threshold you can audit against. "Data must be encrypted" is a policy. "AES-256 must be used" is a standard.

🚗 Think of it like…

Road rules

"Drive safely and without endangering others" is the policy - it states intent and binds everyone, and you cannot measure compliance directly.

"Thirty miles per hour in built-up areas" is the standard - specific, measurable, and enforceable with a camera.

"How to perform an emergency stop" is the procedure - the actual steps, in order.

"It is courteous to signal early" is the guideline. Sensible, recommended, and nobody is prosecuted for ignoring it.

The policies named in the objective

PolicyGoverns
Acceptable use (AUP)What staff may and may not do with organizational systems
Information securityThe overarching security commitments of the organization
Business continuityKeeping the organization operating through disruption
Disaster recoveryRestoring technology after a major failure
Incident responseHow incidents are declared, handled, and reported
Software development lifecycleHow software is built, reviewed, and released securely
Change managementHow changes are requested, approved, and recorded

Note how many of these formalise material from earlier modules. Change management was Module 1, business continuity and disaster recovery were Module 7, and incident response was Module 10. Domain 5 is largely the governance layer above work you have already studied.

The acceptable use policy deserves a specific note because it does something the others do not: it is the document that makes disciplinary action defensible. An organization dismissing someone for misusing systems needs to show they were told what was unacceptable, which is why AUP acknowledgement at onboarding is a control rather than paperwork.

Standards and procedures

The objective names four standard areas - password, access control, physical security, and encryption - and three procedure types: change management, onboarding and offboarding, and playbooks.

Playbooks are worth understanding as a distinct artefact. A playbook is a procedure for a specific scenario: what to do when ransomware is detected, when an account is compromised, when a supplier reports a breach. Their value is that they remove decisions from the moment when people are least able to make them well, which is exactly why Module 10 placed preparation before every other incident response phase.

Onboarding and offboarding procedures connect directly to Module 10's provisioning material. The procedure is what makes deprovisioning happen reliably, and the automation that drives it from HR data is the technical implementation of a governance document.

📕 Think of it like…

A recipe card in the middle of service

A kitchen does not work out how to plate the special while forty covers are waiting. The method was agreed in the morning, written on a card, and pinned where the line can see it - so that at eight o'clock nobody is deciding anything.

A playbook is that card. Its value is not that the steps are clever; it is that the steps were chosen calmly, by people with time to think, and are available at the moment when nobody has any.

Security frameworks

Organizations rarely design a governance programme from nothing, for the same reason Module 8 noted about baselines: published frameworks provide a vetted structure that has survived scrutiny.

FrameworkWhat it provides
NIST Cybersecurity FrameworkFive functions - identify, protect, detect, respond, recover - as an organising structure
ISO/IEC 27001A certifiable information security management system
CIS ControlsA prioritised list of safeguards, ordered by what to do first
COBITGovernance framework linking IT to business objectives

The distinction that matters is between frameworks and regulation. A framework is adopted voluntarily and shapes how a programme is organised; regulation is imposed and must be complied with. Adopting a framework does not itself satisfy a regulator, though it usually makes demonstrating compliance considerably easier.

Notice that the NIST functions map onto this course almost exactly: identify is Domain 2 and asset management, protect is Domains 1 and 3, detect is Module 9's monitoring, and respond and recover are Modules 10 and 7. That is not coincidence - the curriculum is organised around the same lifecycle.

Governance structures

Governance structures

StructureRole
BoardUltimate accountability; sets direction and risk appetite
CommitteeFocused oversight of a domain, reporting upward
Government entityExternal body imposing requirements
Centralized governanceOne authority setting policy for the whole organization
Decentralized governanceBusiness units setting their own within a framework

The centralized and decentralized trade-off mirrors the architectural one from Module 6. Central governance gives consistency and can be slow and remote from local realities. Decentralized governance is responsive and produces divergence, so that two business units solve the same problem differently and one solves it badly.

The board's role is worth stating precisely because exam questions test it: the board sets risk appetite and holds accountability. It does not operate controls, and accountability cannot be delegated to a supplier or an insurer - a point that returns in Module 12.

External considerations and revision

Governance does not happen in isolation. The objective names regulatory, legal, industry, local, regional, national, and global considerations, and their practical effect is that requirements arrive from outside and are not negotiable.

An organization operating internationally faces requirements that conflict - one jurisdiction requiring data retention, another requiring deletion, a third restricting where data may sit at all, as Module 7 described. Resolving those conflicts is governance work rather than technical work.

Monitoring and revision is the element most often skipped. A policy written in 2019 for an office-based workforce, never revisited, now governs a remote organization using cloud services it does not mention. It is not merely out of date - it actively misleads, because staff following it believe they are compliant.

📜 Think of it like…

A staff handbook for a company that no longer exists

A handbook describing the smoking room, the fax rota, and which receptionist holds the stationery key is not simply amusing. Someone following it in good faith is following instructions about a building and a business that are gone.

Unrevised security policy does the same thing more dangerously, because the gap is less obvious. A policy silent on cloud storage does not read as out of date - it reads as permission.

Roles and Responsibilities

The four roles

These are examined precisely, and the definitions do not match casual usage.

RoleResponsibility
Data ownerSenior business figure accountable for a dataset; sets classification and approves access
Data controllerDetermines why and how personal data is processed
Data processorProcesses personal data on the controller's instructions
Data custodian / stewardImplements and maintains the controls the owner requires

The distinction students find hardest is controller versus processor, and it turns on one question: who decides the purpose? A company deciding to run a customer loyalty scheme is the controller. The analytics firm it hires to process that data according to its instructions is the processor. If that firm begins using the data for purposes of its own, it becomes a controller for those purposes - the role follows the decision, not the contract.

Owner versus custodian is a different split: the owner decides, the custodian implements. A finance director owns the payroll data and sets its classification; a database administrator is the custodian who applies encryption and access controls. Security teams are usually custodians, not owners, which is why Module 7 noted that classification decisions belong to the business.

📄 Think of it like…

A client and a printing firm

A charity decides to send a fundraising mailing, chooses who receives it and what it says, and hands the list to a printer. The charity is the controller - it decided the purpose. The printer is the processor - it does exactly what it was instructed and nothing more.

If the printer decides to use that list to advertise its own services, it has stopped acting on instructions and started deciding a purpose. It is now a controller in its own right, with all the obligations that carries, regardless of what the contract says.

Where it breaks down: a printer physically returns the list. A cloud processor retains copies in backups and logs, which is why processor contracts specify deletion and why Module 12 treats vendor agreements as a control.

🏠 Think of it like…

The homeowner and the caretaker

A homeowner decides the house will have a burglar alarm, how sensitive it should be, and who gets a code. A caretaker installs it, tests it monthly, and calls the engineer when it faults.

Both are essential and only one is accountable for the decision. Ask a caretaker to decide who may enter the house and you have moved a judgement to someone who was never given the standing to make it - which is what happens when security teams are asked to classify data they do not own.

Why naming roles matters operationally

Unassigned responsibility is the recurring finding across this entire course. Module 8's unowned asset, Module 9's unattributed risk acceptance, and Module 10's orphaned account are all the same failure in different forms: something existed that nobody was accountable for.

Naming an owner does not by itself protect anything. What it does is ensure that when a decision is needed - approve this access, accept this risk, classify this dataset - there is a specific person whose job it is, rather than a diffuse expectation that someone will notice.

Identifying and Recording Risk

Risk identification and assessment types

Risk identification is finding what could go wrong, drawing on the threat and vulnerability material from Domain 2, audit findings, incident history, and the knowledge of the people who run each system.

Assessment is the evaluation, and the objective names four cadences.

TypeWhen it happens
Ad hocTriggered by an event - an incident, a new threat, an acquisition
RecurringOn a defined schedule, typically annually
One-timeFor a specific project or decision, then complete
ContinuousOngoing, usually tool-driven, reflecting current state

The trend is toward continuous assessment for the reason Module 8 established: environments change constantly, so an annual assessment describes an organization that no longer exists by the time it is published.

The risk register

The register is the organization's list of identified risks and what is being done about each. A usable entry carries more than a description.

ElementPurpose
DescriptionWhat could happen, and what would follow
Likelihood and impactThe two dimensions producing a rating
Risk ownerThe named person accountable for the response
Current controlsWhat already reduces this
StrategyMitigate, transfer, avoid, or accept
Key risk indicatorsMeasures that would show this risk increasing
Review dateWhen this entry is reassessed

Key risk indicators are the element worth understanding rather than memorising. A KRI is a leading measure - something that moves before the risk materialises. The number of unpatched critical vulnerabilities is a KRI for compromise; the number of breaches last quarter is not a KRI at all, because by the time it moves the risk has already occurred.

🚗 Think of it like…

A warning light versus a breakdown

An oil pressure light is a key risk indicator. It moves before the engine seizes, it gives you time to act, and acting on it is cheaper than not.

The number of times you have been towed this year is a fascinating statistic and useless as a warning. It only moves after the thing you were trying to prevent has already happened - which is why counting last quarter's incidents is reporting, not risk indication.

Reading a register entry

Risk tolerance, threshold, and appetite

These three are constantly confused and are individually examinable.

TermMeaning
Risk appetiteHow much risk the organization is willing to take in pursuit of objectives
Risk toleranceHow much variation from that it can actually withstand
Risk thresholdThe specific point at which action becomes required

Appetite is a deliberate strategic choice set by the board. The objective names three postures: expansionary, accepting higher risk for growth; conservative, prioritising stability; and neutral, in between. A startup pursuing market share and a nuclear operator hold genuinely different appetites, and neither is wrong.

Tolerance is about capacity rather than desire. An organization may have an expansionary appetite and a low tolerance for regulatory breach specifically - happy to take commercial risk, unable to withstand losing its licence.

🌶 Think of it like…

How spicy you order versus how spicy you can stand

Appetite is what you choose from the menu. Some people deliberately order the hottest thing available; others never do. It is a preference, and it is a decision made in advance.

Tolerance is what you can actually get through without a problem. These are different numbers, and people get into difficulty precisely when appetite exceeds tolerance - which is also how organizations get into difficulty.

Analysing Risk

📋 Think of it like…

A to-do list with nobody's name on it

A shared household list reading 'fix the gate, chase the insurance, sort the damp' produces nothing. Everyone has read it, everyone assumes someone else is on it, and in a month it reads the same.

Add a name and a date to each line and the same list starts moving. A risk register without owners and review dates is that first list: an accurate description of what is wrong, and no mechanism whatsoever for it to change.

Qualitative and quantitative

QualitativeQuantitative
Expresses risk asCategories - high, medium, lowMonetary values
RequiresJudgement and experienceData on asset values and frequencies
StrengthFast, works where data is absentComparable, supports cost-benefit decisions
WeaknessSubjective; two assessors may disagreePrecision can be spurious if inputs are guessed

Most organizations use both: qualitative to triage the whole population quickly, quantitative on the significant few where the numbers justify the effort. The exam's concern is that you know which is which and what each is for.

The caution about quantitative analysis is worth taking seriously. A calculation producing £847,500 looks authoritative, and if the frequency input was a guess, the output is that guess with decimal places. The confidence a number projects is not evidence about the number.

The quantitative formulas

These appear on the exam as calculations, and they are the only arithmetic in the course.

TermMeaningFormula
AVAsset value—
EFExposure factor - proportion of the asset lost in one event—
SLESingle loss expectancy - cost of one occurrenceAV × EF
AROAnnualised rate of occurrence - times per year—
ALEAnnualised loss expectancy - expected annual costSLE × ARO

An ARO below one expresses an event less frequent than annual: once every four years is 0.25. This is where arithmetic slips happen under exam pressure, so read the frequency carefully before converting.

The purpose of ALE is comparison against control cost. If a risk carries an ALE of £40,000 and a control costing £15,000 a year would eliminate it, the control is justified. If the control costs £90,000, it is not - and spending it anyway is a worse decision than accepting the risk, however uncomfortable that sounds.

🌊 Think of it like…

Costing a flood

Your ground floor and its contents are worth £200,000. A flood ruins about a quarter of that - the exposure factor is 0.25 - so one flood costs £50,000. That is the single loss expectancy.

Historically the river floods roughly once every ten years, so the annualised rate of occurrence is 0.1. Multiply, and flooding costs you £5,000 a year on average - the annualised loss expectancy.

Now the decision is answerable. A £2,000-a-year flood barrier is worth it. A £12,000-a-year one is not, and the fact that flooding feels frightening does not change the arithmetic.

⚖️ Think of it like…

Two ways to be told a number

A surveyor saying 'the roof is in poor condition' and one saying '£18,000 to replace, likely needed within three years' have told you about the same roof. Only the second lets you compare it against the kitchen, the boiler, and what you actually have.

That is qualitative against quantitative. The first is quick and adequate for triage; the second is what you need when two things are competing for the same money.

Where it breaks down: a surveyor's estimate rests on inspection. A frequency figure often rests on a guess - and £847,500 looks equally authoritative either way, which is the trap.

Probability, likelihood, and impact

The objective lists these separately from the formulas. Probability is a mathematical expression of chance, likelihood is a qualitative judgement of the same idea, and impact is the consequence if it happens.

Risk combines likelihood and impact, and neither alone is sufficient. A near-certain event with trivial impact and a catastrophic event that is essentially impossible both produce low risk, for opposite reasons. The entries that need attention are those scoring meaningfully on both axes.

Strategies, Reporting, and Business Impact

The four risk management strategies

StrategyWhat it doesExample
MitigateReduce likelihood or impact with controlsPatch, segment, add MFA
TransferMove financial consequence to another partyInsurance, contractual terms
AvoidStop doing the thing that creates the riskDiscontinue a service, decline a market
AcceptTake no further action, deliberately and on recordDocumented exception with an owner

Three points recur in exam questions. Transfer moves financial consequence, never accountability - an insured organization still faces the regulator and still owes its customers an explanation. Avoidance is genuinely available and students forget it; sometimes the correct answer is not to offer the service. And acceptance is legitimate when documented with a named acceptor and a review date, which is the Module 9 exception in governance language.

🏠 Think of it like…

Four responses to a house on a floodplain

Avoid - do not buy the house. The risk never enters your life.

Mitigate - buy it and fit flood barriers and raised sockets. The water may still come; less of it, and it costs less.

Transfer - buy flood insurance. The water arrives exactly as before and someone else pays for the carpets. Note what does not transfer: you still have nowhere to sleep on Tuesday.

Accept - buy it, know the risk, keep a mop, and decide the location is worth it. That is a decision, not negligence, provided it was actually made.

🏥 Think of it like…

Consent before an operation

A surgeon explaining a procedure does not read out the whole file. They state what is being proposed, what could go wrong, how likely that is, and what decision is being asked of the patient right now.

Risk reporting to a board is the same act. The register is the file. What the board needs is the significant risks, whether things are improving, what sits outside appetite, and which decisions are theirs to make.

Risk reporting

Reporting communicates the risk position to those accountable for it, and Module 9's lesson about audience applies. A board needs the significant risks, whether the position is improving, which risks sit outside appetite, and what decisions are being asked of them. It does not need the register.

The most important content is usually the exceptions: risks that have been accepted, by whom, and when they will be reviewed. That is the list the board is accountable for and the one most likely to be absent from a report focused on activity.

Business impact analysis

A business impact analysis determines which functions matter most and how quickly they must be restored. It is the input to the continuity and recovery decisions from Module 7 - the BIA establishes the requirement, and the architecture satisfies it.

MetricMeaningSet by
RTOMaximum acceptable time to restore a functionThe business
RPOMaximum acceptable data lossThe business
MTTRMean time to repair - average actual recovery timeMeasured
MTBFMean time between failures - average interval between themMeasured

The distinction that matters is which are targets and which are measurements. RTO and RPO are requirements the business states. MTTR and MTBF are observations of how systems actually behave. Comparing them is the useful act: an MTTR of 30 hours against an RTO of 4 hours is a gap requiring investment, and you cannot know that without both numbers.

🔧 Think of it like…

A delivery van's service record

MTBF is how long the van typically runs between breakdowns - roughly eight months. MTTR is how long it usually takes to get back on the road - about three days.

Those are facts about the van. RTO is different in kind: it is the business saying deliveries cannot stop for more than one day. Comparing them gives you the decision - three days of actual repair against one day of tolerance means you need a spare van, a faster mechanic, or a revised promise to customers.

Reading BIA metrics together

Inherent, residual, and control risk

Three related terms describe risk at different points in the treatment process, and exam questions use them to test whether you know which stage is being discussed.

TermMeaning
Inherent riskThe risk before any controls are applied
Residual riskWhat remains after controls are in place
Control riskThe risk that a control itself fails or is ineffective

Residual risk is the figure that matters operationally, because it is what the organization actually carries and therefore what acceptance decisions apply to. A register recording inherent risk alone describes a world in which the organization has done nothing.

Control risk is the one students overlook, and it is the reason validation exists throughout this course. The untested backup from Module 7, the untuned alert rule from Module 9, and the patch deployed but never confirmed from Module 9 are all control risk: a control was counted on and did not deliver, so the residual risk was understated all along.

What a BIA produces

What a BIA produces

Beyond the metrics, a BIA identifies which business functions are critical, what each depends on, and the order in which they must be restored. That dependency mapping is where BIAs earn their cost: an organization frequently discovers that a function it rated non-critical is a dependency of one it rated essential, so restoring them in priority order would not work.

The other common finding is that stated RTOs are unaffordable in aggregate. Every department believes its function is critical, and totalling the resulting requirements produces a cost nobody will fund - which forces the genuinely useful conversation about relative priority that the BIA existed to prompt.

[EXAM] Three discriminators. A standard is measurable and a policy states intent. Appetite is what you are willing to take; tolerance is what you can withstand. And RTO and RPO are targets set by the business, while MTTR and MTBF are measurements of what actually happens.
