📋 Overview

Where incident response becomes evidence.

Digital forensics is the disciplined recovery and analysis of evidence. In Autopsy you'll create a case, load a disk image as the evidence source, explore the file system (including recovering deleted files), tag and comment on findings, and generate a report. The constant underneath every step is integrity: you work on a copy, you prove it hasn't changed, and you document everything.

[THE ONE IDEA] Work on a copy, prove it's unchanged. You examine a hashed image, never the original drive, so the evidence stays pristine and your findings are defensible. Preservation first, analysis second.

⚠️ Where students get stuck

Forensic discipline, where it's easy to slip.

[IMAGE, NOT ORIGINAL] You examine a disk image, never the original drive. Working on the original would alter evidence and destroy its integrity — this is the cardinal rule.
[HASHING PROVES INTEGRITY] A forensic image is hashed; matching hashes prove it wasn't altered. If the hashes differ, the evidence is compromised. (The hashing lab's avalanche effect is exactly why this works.)
['DELETED' ISN'T GONE] Recovered files surprise people — deletion typically removes the file's pointer, not its data, until overwritten. That's what makes recovery (and secure deletion) possible.
[TAGS + COMMENTS ARE THE RECORD] Tagging without comments loses the 'why.' The documented reasoning is what makes a finding usable later.
[THE REPORT IS THE POINT] The analysis isn't done until it's documented in a report. Findings in your head aren't evidence.

🛡️ The defender's view

Forensics is governed by integrity and accountability more than by any tool.

Image & hash the evidence

Breaks: altered originals

Acquire a bit-for-bit image and hash it. Work only on the copy; the hash proves at any point that the evidence is unchanged — integrity you can demonstrate.

Chain of custody

Breaks: inadmissible evidence

Document who handled the evidence, when, and what they did, start to finish. A broken custody chain can make sound findings worthless in a proceeding.

Work on copies only

Breaks: destroying the original

The original is preserved and sealed; all analysis happens on verified copies. One examination on the original can taint everything.

Document every action

Breaks: unreproducible findings

Forensics must be reproducible — another examiner should reach the same result. Tags, comments, and the report are that documentation.

Timeline analysis

Breaks: missing the story

Ordering artifacts by time reconstructs what happened and when — turning scattered files into a narrative of the incident.

Secure deletion (the flip side)

Breaks: recoverable 'deleted' data

Because 'delete' leaves data behind, truly removing sensitive data needs wiping or crypto-shredding — the defensive lesson of how recovery works.

✅ Check yourself

1. Why do forensic examiners work on a disk image rather than the original drive?

Any action on the original alters it and destroys the evidence's integrity. Working on a bit-for-bit image preserves the original untouched while allowing full analysis on the copy.

2. How is the integrity of an evidence image proven?

By hashing it (e.g. SHA-256) at acquisition and comparing hashes later. Because a hash avalanches on any change, matching values prove the image is byte-identical and unaltered — the technical backbone of chain of custody.

3. Autopsy recovers files a user 'deleted.' How is that possible, and what's the defensive lesson?

Deleting a file usually just removes its directory pointer, leaving the data blocks intact until overwritten, so they can be recovered. The defensive lesson: truly removing sensitive data requires secure wiping or crypto-shredding, not ordinary deletion.

4. Why are tagging, comments, and the report so central to forensics?

Forensics must be documented and reproducible. Tags and comments capture what was found and why; the report compiles it into an accountable, repeatable account — which is what makes the findings defensible to IR, management, or a court.
