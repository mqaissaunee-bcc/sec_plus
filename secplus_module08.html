Secure Baselines, Hardening, and Asset Management

Establishing and maintaining secure baselines, hardening every class of target, wireless and mobile deployment, application security controls, and managing assets from procurement to certified disposal.

Progress: 0% complete

Overview

[OBJECTIVES] By the end of this module you will be able to explain how a secure baseline is established, deployed, and maintained; select hardening techniques appropriate to each class of target; plan wireless installation and choose wireless security settings; compare mobile deployment models and the role of MDM; apply application security controls including code signing and sandboxing; and manage assets from acquisition through to certified disposal.

Domain 4 is Security Operations at 28% - the largest domain on the exam and the one closest to daily practice. This module covers the foundation: configuring systems securely in the first place, and knowing what you own.

The two objectives pair for a reason that is easy to miss. Objective 4.1 is about applying security techniques to computing resources, and objective 4.2 is about asset management. You cannot apply a baseline to a system you do not know exists, so asset management is not an administrative afterthought appended to the technical work - it is the precondition for it.

The theme running through Domain 4

Earlier domains asked what could go wrong and how to design against it. Domain 4 asks how you keep a large estate in a known-good state over time, which is a different problem. A single server can be configured perfectly by someone careful. Ten thousand endpoints cannot, and the difference is not effort but method.

Almost everything in this module is an answer to one question: how do you make correct configuration the default and detect when reality has diverged from it? Baselines define the known-good state, hardening decides what that state contains, configuration enforcement holds systems to it, and asset management ensures nothing is excluded because nobody knew it was there.

🛠 Think of it like…

Building code versus a careful builder

One skilled builder constructing one house can make sound judgements at every step. That approach does not scale to a thousand houses built by four hundred people over a decade.

Building codes solve this. The standard is written once, inspected against, and revised as materials and knowledge change. The result is not that every house is perfect - it is that no house is wrong in a way nobody noticed, which is the achievable goal at scale.

Secure Baselines

What a baseline is

A secure baseline is a documented, approved configuration standard for a class of system - every Windows workstation, every Linux web server, every network switch. It specifies which services run, which settings apply, which accounts exist, and what is explicitly disabled.

The value is that it converts security from a judgement made repeatedly into a standard applied consistently. A configuration decision is made once, reviewed properly, and then applied everywhere - rather than being reinvented, slightly differently, by whoever builds the next server.

Establish, deploy, maintain

Objective 4.1 names three phases, and each fails in a characteristic way.

PhaseWhat happensHow it fails
EstablishDefine the standard, usually from an industry benchmark, adjusted for the environmentWritten by security alone, breaks applications, gets exceptions until it means nothing
DeployApply it to systems, ideally at build time through automationApplied only to new systems, so the existing estate never converges
MaintainUpdate it as threats and software change; detect and correct driftWritten once and never revisited, so it slowly becomes wrong

The establish phase deserves particular care. A baseline written without the teams who run the applications will break something, and the response is always an exception. Enough exceptions and the baseline documents an aspiration rather than a configuration.

Benchmarks: not starting from scratch

Organizations rarely invent baselines. Published benchmarks provide a vetted starting point.

SourceWhat it provides
CIS BenchmarksConsensus configuration guidance for common platforms, with graded levels
DISA STIGsSecurity technical implementation guides used in US defence environments
Vendor guidanceThe platform maker's own hardening recommendations

These are starting points rather than answers. A benchmark cannot know that a particular service is essential to your business, so a baseline is the benchmark adjusted for your environment - with each deviation documented and justified, which is what distinguishes a considered exception from drift.

📜 Think of it like…

A published recipe you adjust

A professional recipe from a trusted source has been tested by people who understand the chemistry. Starting there is faster and safer than inventing a method, and it does not oblige you to follow it blindly - you adjust for your oven and the ingredients you can actually get.

The difference between an adjustment and a mistake is that the adjustment is deliberate and written down. That is exactly the difference between a documented baseline exception and configuration drift.

Configuration drift

Drift is the gradual divergence of real systems from their defined baseline. It happens through emergency fixes never reversed, manual troubleshooting changes, software installing its own settings, and undocumented adjustments accumulating over years.

Drift is dangerous because it is invisible without deliberate checking. Systems continue working, so nothing prompts investigation, and the organization's understanding of its own configuration slowly becomes fiction. When an incident occurs, responders act on documentation that no longer describes reality - the same failure as the stale network diagram in Module 1.

Configuration enforcement is the answer: tooling that periodically compares actual state against the baseline and either alerts or automatically corrects. The infrastructure-as-code approach from Module 6 goes further by rebuilding systems from a definition rather than modifying them, so drift has less opportunity to accumulate.

🏠 Think of it like…

A house that no longer matches its plans

Over twenty years a wall comes down, a socket is added behind a cupboard, and a doorway is boarded over during a leak and never reopened. Each change was reasonable on the day and none was drawn onto the plans.

The house works. Nothing prompts anyone to check. Then a fire officer arrives with the original drawings and plans an escape route through a doorway that has not existed since 2011.

That is configuration drift, and it is why the danger is not any single undocumented change - it is that the organization's understanding of itself has quietly become fiction.

Hardening Targets and Techniques

The techniques

Module 5 introduced hardening in the mitigation catalogue. Here the question is which techniques apply to which class of system.

Disable unused ports and protocolsListening services nobody needs
Change default credentialsThe simplest vector in the curriculum
Least functionalityEvery capability not required by the role
Host-based firewallControl of traffic to and from the host itself

🔌 Think of it like…

Unplugging what you never use

A house accumulates devices plugged in permanently - a printer used twice a year, a games console nobody plays, a hi-fi behind the sofa. Each draws power, each can fail, and each is one more thing that could go wrong while nobody is watching it.

Hardening is walking round and unplugging the things nobody uses. It removes no capability anyone wanted, and it shrinks what has to be maintained and watched - which is why least functionality is a security principle rather than mere tidiness.

Targets differ, and so do the techniques

Objective 4.1 lists hardening targets explicitly, and the exam tests whether you know that the same technique list does not apply uniformly.

TargetEmphasisWhat is often impossible
WorkstationsEDR, disk encryption, patching, least privilege-
ServersLeast functionality, baselines, host firewall-
Mobile devicesMDM, encryption, remote wipe, app vettingFull local control on personal devices
Switches and routersChange defaults, disable unused ports, secure management planeInstalling endpoint agents
Cloud infrastructureIdentity policy, configuration scanning, loggingAccess to layers the provider operates
ICS / SCADASegmentation, strict access, passive monitoringPatching, agents, active scanning
Embedded and RTOSNetwork isolation, vendor firmware updatesAgents, and often any update at all
IoT devicesChange defaults, isolate on a separate networkPatching, agents, sometimes even inventory

The right-hand column is where exam questions live. For workstations and servers the full toolkit applies. For everything below them, some techniques are unavailable, and the answer shifts toward controls around the device - segmentation, access restriction, monitoring - which is the compensating control pattern from Module 1 appearing for the fourth time.

Note also the one technique that applies to every row without exception: changing default credentials. It requires no agent, no patch, and no vendor support, which is why it remains the highest-return control available on the devices that are hardest to secure.

🚲 Think of it like…

Securing a bicycle and a lorry

You secure a bicycle with a good lock and by not leaving it out overnight. You secure a lorry with an immobiliser, a tracker, a depot with a barrier, and a driver vetting process. Both are vehicles; almost nothing transfers between them.

Asking "how do I harden this?" without asking what it is produces the wrong answer. A plant controller and a laptop are both computers, and the list of what you can actually do to each barely overlaps.

Hardening the management plane

Network devices, hypervisors, cloud consoles, and storage arrays share a property that makes them disproportionately valuable: the interface used to administer them controls everything beneath. Compromising a switch's management interface is not equivalent to compromising one host - it is control over what every host on that switch can reach.

The standard measures follow from that. Management interfaces belong on a dedicated network reachable only from a jump server, as in Module 6. Administrative accounts should be individual rather than shared, so accounting can attribute actions. Default credentials must be changed before the device is connected rather than afterwards, because internet scanning finds newly exposed services within minutes. And unused management protocols - older, unencrypted remote access in particular - should be disabled rather than merely deprioritised, for the downgrade reasons covered in Module 5.

Hardening cloud infrastructure

Cloud hardening looks different because the layers you would normally configure belong to the provider. The customer's work concentrates in three places, all of which sit on the customer side of the shared responsibility line from Module 4.

AreaWhat hardening means here
Identity and permissionsLeast privilege on both human and machine identities; no standing broad policies
ConfigurationAutomated scanning against a baseline, since misconfiguration is the leading cloud finding
LoggingEnabled at account creation, exported outside the account, retained for investigation

The logging point is easy to underrate. Cloud logging is frequently disabled by default and, once enabled, often written into the same account being investigated - so an attacker with sufficient permissions can delete the record of what they did. Exporting logs to a separate account with different credentials is the cloud version of forwarding logs off the host.

Wireless and Mobile

Installation: site surveys and heat maps

Wireless security begins before any configuration, with where the access points go. A site survey measures actual signal behaviour in the physical space - how walls, metal, and machinery attenuate signal, and what existing networks are already present. A heat map is the visual output, showing signal strength across a floor plan.

The security relevance is direct. Under-provisioned coverage leaves dead spots where users connect to whatever they can find, which is exactly the condition a rogue access point exploits. Over-provisioned coverage spills signal into the car park and the neighbouring building, extending the attack surface to anyone who can sit within range.

A survey also reveals interference and channel overlap, which matter for availability - and availability is a security property.

💡 Think of it like…

Planning the lighting for a building

You do not fit lights by guessing. You walk the space, note where the windows are and where the shadows fall, and place fixtures so every area is lit without floodlighting the neighbours at three in the morning.

A site survey is that walk. Too little coverage and people improvise their own light source, which is the rogue access point. Too much and you are illuminating the car park for anyone who cares to look.

Wireless security settings

SettingWhat it provides
WPA3Current standard; stronger handshake resisting offline password attacks
WPA2Previous standard, still widespread, weaker handshake
WEPLong broken; always the wrong answer
AAA / RADIUSCentral authentication rather than a shared network password
EAP variantsThe framework carrying credentials - passwords, certificates, tokens

The distinction the exam tests most is personal versus enterprise mode. Personal uses one pre-shared key everyone knows, so an employee leaving means the key is compromised until it is changed on every device. Enterprise authenticates each user individually against a RADIUS server, so an individual can be revoked without touching anyone else.

WPA3's principal improvement is worth understanding rather than memorising. Under WPA2, an attacker who captures the handshake can attempt passwords offline at unlimited speed. WPA3's handshake removes that possibility, so a weak passphrase is no longer catastrophic in the same way - each guess must be made against the live network.

🔑 Think of it like…

A shared door code versus individual badges

A door code everyone knows is simple and works well until someone leaves. Then the code is compromised, and the only remedy is telling all two hundred people a new one - so in practice nobody changes it, and the code circulates for years.

Individual badges cost more to run and let you revoke exactly one person in ten seconds. That is the difference between WPA personal and enterprise, and it is why enterprise is the answer whenever a scenario mentions staff turnover.

Mobile deployment models

Three models, distinguished by who owns the device and how much control the organization has.

ModelOwnershipControlMain tension
BYODEmployeeLowestPrivacy; the organization cannot wipe a personal device wholesale
COPEOrganizationHighestCost, and personal use of a monitored device
CYODOrganizationHighChoice within an approved list; a compromise on both

BYOD is the model that generates exam scenarios, because the conflict is genuine. The organization needs to protect corporate data on a device it does not own, while the employee has a legitimate expectation of privacy over their photographs, messages, and location.

The resolution is containerization: corporate data and applications are kept in a managed, encrypted container on the device. The organization can wipe the container when someone leaves without touching personal data, and cannot see outside it. This is why a question describing BYOD plus a need to remove corporate data almost always points to selective or container wipe rather than a full device wipe.

🚗 Think of it like…

Whose car you drive for work

BYOD is using your own car and claiming mileage. The company cannot fit a tracker to it or tell you what to keep in the boot, but it does care that the client files on the back seat are secure.

COPE is a company car you may also use personally. The company owns it, sets the rules, and knows where it has been - and you accept that in exchange for not buying a car.

CYOD is a company car chosen from an approved list. You get some say in what you drive, and the fleet stays small enough to maintain properly.

Mobile device management

MDM is the tooling that enforces policy on mobile devices: requiring encryption and a passcode, deploying and restricting applications, separating corporate data into a container, and wiping remotely.

Connection methods each carry their own exposure. Cellular is generally the most controlled path. Wi-Fi brings the rogue access point and evil twin risks from Module 5, particularly on public networks. Bluetooth introduces pairing and discovery attacks, and its practical weakness is that users leave it discoverable indefinitely.

Application Security, Sandboxing, and Monitoring

Module 4 covered these as defences against specific vulnerabilities. Objective 4.1 asks about them operationally - as controls applied across an estate rather than fixes for one flaw.

Input validation and secure cookies

Input validation was established in Module 4 as the boundary decision: determine what input is acceptable and treat everything else as data. Operationally, the point is that it must be applied server-side. Client-side validation improves the user experience and provides no security whatever, because an attacker simply does not use your client.

Secure cookies are a specific and testable set of attributes.

AttributeEffect
SecureSent only over HTTPS, never in the clear
HttpOnlyNot readable by client-side script, limiting XSS damage
SameSiteRestricts sending on cross-site requests, mitigating CSRF

Notice how these map onto Module 4: HttpOnly limits what a successful XSS can steal, and SameSite addresses the CSRF pattern where the browser helpfully attaches cookies to a request the user never intended.

Static and dynamic analysis

MethodExaminesFindsMisses
Static (SAST)Source code, not runningFlaw patterns, early in developmentRuntime and configuration issues; produces false positives
Dynamic (DAST)The running applicationReal behaviour, configuration flawsCode paths not exercised during the test

They are complementary rather than competing. Static analysis runs early and cheaply and flags things that are not actually exploitable. Dynamic analysis confirms real behaviour and only sees what it manages to reach. An organization relying on one alone has a predictable blind spot.

Code signing

Code signing applies a digital signature to software so recipients can verify who published it and that it has not been altered since. It is the Module 2 digital signature applied to executables, and it underpins the application allow lists from Module 5 - a policy permitting only signed code from approved publishers is far more maintainable than one listing individual files.

Its limit is worth stating precisely, because exam options exploit the confusion. A signature proves the software came from the holder of that signing key and has not changed. It proves nothing about whether the software is safe, and nothing about whether the publisher was compromised when they signed it - which is exactly the supply chain attack from Module 3.

✉ Think of it like…

A wax seal on a letter

An intact seal bearing a known crest tells you two things reliably: who sent the letter, and that nobody opened it on the way. Both are genuinely useful and neither is a small thing.

What the seal does not tell you is whether the contents are true, or whether someone had already got into the sender's study and swapped the pages before it was sealed. Code signing has exactly these limits - which is why signature verification and behavioural monitoring after installation are both required.

Sandboxing

Sandboxing runs code in a constrained environment where it cannot reach the rest of the system. It appears in three operational forms: detonating suspicious attachments to observe behaviour, isolating browser tabs and applications from each other and the OS, and separating development and test environments from production.

Sandboxing is detection and containment rather than prevention. Some malware detects that it is being observed and stays dormant, so a clean sandbox result is weaker evidence than it appears - which is why sandbox analysis complements endpoint monitoring rather than replacing it.

🌱 Think of it like…

A quarantine greenhouse

A botanical garden receiving an unfamiliar plant does not put it straight into the main collection. It goes into a separate greenhouse with its own soil, water, and air, and is watched for a season to see what it does.

If it carries a pest, the pest is contained. But a plant that lies dormant through the observation period and only spreads later has beaten the quarantine - which is precisely how sandbox-aware malware behaves, and why the main collection is still monitored.

🔍 Think of it like…

A safety inspection with no follow-up visits

A building passes inspection on the day it opens. The certificate goes in a drawer and nobody returns for six years, during which extensions are built, fire doors are propped open, and the alarm is disconnected for redecorating.

The certificate is still accurate about the day it was issued and says nothing about the building now. A hardened system nobody watches is exactly that certificate: a snapshot of a good decision rather than an ongoing control.

Monitoring as a hardening control

Monitoring appears in objective 4.1 alongside configuration controls because a hardened system that nobody watches is a snapshot of a good decision rather than an ongoing control. Systems drift, new software is installed, and accounts are added.

What matters operationally is that logging must be enabled at build time as part of the baseline, forwarded off the host so a compromise cannot erase it, and retained long enough to investigate an incident that may have begun months earlier. Module 5's missing-logs indicator is the consequence of getting the second point wrong.

Asset Management

Objective 4.2 covers the full asset lifecycle. This is the least glamorous content in Domain 4 and among the most consequential, because every control in this module presumes you know the asset exists.

Acquisition and procurement

Security begins before a device is bought. Procurement is where vendor assessment happens, where support and patching commitments are agreed, and where end-of-support dates are known rather than discovered years later.

The question most often skipped is how long the vendor will issue security updates. A device purchased with three years of support and used for eight spends five years unpatchable - the end-of-life problem from Module 4, created at purchase by nobody asking.

📖 Think of it like…

A library book with no borrower's name

A book checked out under a name has someone responsible for returning it, and the library knows who to ask. A book that left the shelf with no record is simply gone - nobody is at fault, nobody is chasing it, and nobody notices it never came back.

An asset without a named owner is that book. It is nobody's to patch, nobody's to decommission, and nobody's to answer for when it turns up in a scan - which is why ownership is what makes every other control work.

Assignment and accounting

Every asset needs an owner - a named person or team accountable for it - and a classification reflecting the sensitivity of what it processes, which connects to Module 7.

Ownership is what makes everything else work. An unowned asset is nobody's to patch, nobody's to decommission, and nobody's to answer for when it appears in a scan. Organizations discovering forgotten servers are discovering assets that lost their owner during a reorganisation and were never reassigned.

What an inventory record needs to contain

An inventory listing only device names and serial numbers cannot support the decisions that depend on it. A usable record carries enough to answer the questions that arise during an incident or an audit.

FieldThe question it answers
OwnerWho is accountable, and who approves changes?
ClassificationHow sensitive is what this processes?
LocationPhysical site, or cloud region for sovereignty
Software and versionsIs this affected by the vulnerability announced today?
End-of-support dateWhen does this become unpatchable?
DependenciesWhat breaks if this is decommissioned?

The software field is what turns an inventory from a finance record into a security control. When a vulnerability is announced in a widely used component, the immediate question is which systems contain it - and an organization that cannot answer within hours spends days searching while the exploitation window from Module 4 stays open.

Monitoring and tracking

Monitoring and tracking

An inventory is the record of what the organization owns. Enumeration is actively discovering what is present. The gap between them is the interesting part: assets on the network that appear in no inventory, and inventory entries for assets that no longer exist.

Both directions matter. Undiscovered assets are unmanaged and unpatched. Phantom inventory entries waste effort and create false assurance that something has been decommissioned when it is still running somewhere.

📦 Think of it like…

Stocktaking in a warehouse

The system says four hundred units. Counting the shelves finds three hundred and eighty on the system's shelves, plus sixty in a corner nobody has looked at since a delivery two years ago.

Both discrepancies are problems and they are different problems. The missing twenty are somewhere unaccounted for. The unexpected sixty are stock the business has been carrying, insuring, and never selling - and in the network equivalent, never patching.

Disposal and decommissioning

The end of the lifecycle is where data most often escapes, because the device leaves the organization's control while its storage does not necessarily leave with its data removed.

MethodWhat it doesAppropriate for
DeletionRemoves the reference; data remains until overwrittenNothing sensitive
SanitizationOverwrites or cryptographically erases so data is unrecoverableReusable media
DestructionPhysically destroys the media - shredding, degaussing, incinerationHighly sensitive data, failed media
CertificationDocumented evidence that sanitization or destruction occurredRegulated data, audit requirements

Two points recur in exam questions. Deletion is not sanitization - a deleted file is generally still present until overwritten, which is why drives sold second-hand routinely yield recoverable data. And certification matters because it is the evidence: without a certificate of destruction, an organization cannot demonstrate to a regulator that data on a disposed device was actually removed.

Failed media deserves specific mention. A drive too damaged to overwrite cannot be sanitized, so destruction is the only option - and a failed drive returned to the vendor under warranty leaves with its data intact unless that is negotiated in advance.

🗑 Think of it like…

Throwing out documents versus shredding them

Putting papers in the bin removes them from your desk. Anyone going through the bin has them, complete and readable, because nothing about being in the bin altered the paper.

Shredding destroys the content. And for a legal record you want the collection receipt from the shredding company - not because you doubt them, but because when someone asks in two years what happened to those files, "we shredded them" and a certificate are different answers.

Choosing a disposal method

Retention

Data retention determines how long records are kept before disposal, and as Module 7 established, requirements pull in two directions: regulation mandating minimum periods, and privacy law requiring that personal data not be kept longer than necessary.

Decommissioning must respect both. Destroying a device holding records subject to a legal hold is a serious problem, and retaining data long past its purpose is liability with no benefit. This is why disposal is a checklist item in a documented process rather than a decision made by whoever is clearing the store cupboard.

[EXAM] Three discriminators. Deletion is not sanitization. Certification is the evidence, and scenarios mentioning regulators or audits want it named. And when a hardening technique is impossible - agents on ICS, patching on IoT - the answer moves to controls around the device.
