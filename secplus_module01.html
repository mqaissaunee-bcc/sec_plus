Security Fundamentals and Controls

Overview

[OBJECTIVES] By the end of this module you will be able to explain the CIA triad and non-repudiation; distinguish authentication, authorization, and accounting; classify a security control by both category and type; describe the zero trust model and the split between control plane and data plane; and explain why change management is a security process rather than a paperwork exercise.

Security+ is a vocabulary exam before it is a technical one. Roughly a third of the questions you will miss on a practice test are not questions about technology you do not understand - they are questions where two answer choices are both technically true and only one matches the precise term CompTIA is asking about. This module builds that vocabulary.

The concepts here map to exam objectives 1.1 through 1.3, which together account for the bulk of the General Security Concepts domain (12% of the exam). That understates their real weight. The control taxonomy in particular reappears throughout Domains 3, 4, and 5: when a later question asks you to select the best mitigation for a scenario, it is testing whether you can classify controls.

How this module is organized

Five teaching sections, then an assessment. The first two sections cover the goals of security (what we are protecting, and how we know who is acting). The middle two cover the means (what controls exist, and how modern architectures decide whom to trust). The last covers the process that keeps all of it from decaying over time.

Why this module carries more weight than 12%

Domain 1 is the smallest domain by exam weight, and students routinely treat it as a warm-up to get past on the way to the technical material. That is a mistake, and it shows up in practice-test results in a specific way: candidates score well on Domain 1 questions and then lose points across Domains 3, 4, and 5 on questions that are secretly Domain 1 questions wearing different clothes.

Consider a Domain 4 question that describes a SIEM alerting on anomalous logins and asks what kind of control the SIEM represents. The subject matter is security operations, but the skill being tested is the control taxonomy from this module. Or a Domain 5 question describing a vendor who cannot meet an encryption requirement and asks what the organization should implement instead. That is third-party risk on the surface and compensating controls underneath.

The vocabulary you build here is the machinery you will use to answer questions for the rest of the course. Learn it precisely now and the rest of the exam gets easier. Learn it approximately and you will keep losing points without understanding why.

[EXAM] Watch for questions that give a scenario and ask for the control type rather than the control itself. "A security guard" is a physical control by category, but it can be deterrent, preventive, or detective depending on what the scenario says the guard is doing.

The CIA Triad and Non-Repudiation

Every security control that exists is ultimately protecting one of three properties, and most protect one at the expense of another. The triad is not a checklist to satisfy - it is a set of competing goals you are constantly balancing.

🏦 Think of it like…

A safe deposit box at a bank

The bank protects confidentiality - only you and the box's authorised signatories can see what is inside. It protects integrity because nobody can quietly swap your documents for forgeries. And it protects availability because you can walk in during business hours and retrieve your things.

Now notice the tension. The bank could make the box more confidential by requiring two forms of ID, a co-signer, and 48 hours' notice. Every one of those makes your documents harder for a thief to reach - and harder for you to reach. That is the trade-off in miniature, and it is the same trade-off behind every security decision you will study.

Where it breaks down: a bank vault has one physical location. Data can be copied perfectly and instantly, so a confidentiality failure means the information is now in two places at once, and you cannot take it back.

Confidentiality

Information is available only to those authorized to see it. The primary technical mechanism is encryption, supported by access control and, in transit, by protocols like TLS. Confidentiality failures are the ones that make the news: a database of customer records exposed to the internet, a laptop lost with unencrypted disk, an employee emailing a spreadsheet to a personal account.

Integrity

Information has not been altered by anyone unauthorized to alter it, whether maliciously or accidentally. The primary mechanism is hashing: a cryptographic hash of a file computed before and after transmission will differ if even one bit changed. Digital signatures extend this by binding the integrity check to an identity.

Integrity failures are quieter than confidentiality failures and often more damaging. A breach that exposes records is bad; a breach that silently modifies financial records over months, leaving you unable to say which numbers are true, can be worse.

Availability

Information and systems are accessible when authorized users need them. Mechanisms include redundancy, failover, backups, and capacity planning. Availability is the property most often traded away by the other two: every additional authentication step, every encryption operation, every integrity check adds latency or a new failure mode.

[NOTE] A system that is perfectly confidential and perfectly integral but offline provides zero security value. Availability is a security property, not an operational afterthought - which is why disaster recovery sits inside the security curriculum.

The tension between the three

Exam scenarios frequently describe an organization that has over- rotated on one property and ask what they have sacrificed. Reading the scenario for which property is being traded away is usually faster than reasoning about the control itself.

The three states of data

Confidentiality controls differ depending on where the data is at the moment you protect it, and the exam expects you to match the state to the mechanism.

StateMeaningTypical protection
At restStored on disk, in a database, on backup media Full-disk or file-level encryption, access control
In transitMoving across a network TLS, IPsec, VPN tunnels
In useLoaded in memory and being processed Trusted execution environments, memory encryption

Data in use is the hardest of the three and the one most often overlooked. To compute on data, a processor generally has to see it in plaintext, which means an attacker with sufficient access to a running system can read it regardless of how well it was encrypted on disk or over the wire. This is why scenarios describing memory-scraping malware are describing an attack on data in use, and why the standard answers involve hardware-backed enclaves rather than more encryption.

✉️ Think of it like…

A letter you are sending

At rest, the letter sits in a locked drawer. In transit, it travels in a sealed envelope through the postal system. In use, someone has opened it and is reading it aloud in a room.

You can lock the drawer and seal the envelope, but you cannot protect the letter while it is being read - the words have to be visible to whoever is reading them. That is exactly the problem with data in use: a processor generally needs plaintext to compute on, so the protection has to come from controlling the room rather than the paper.

Where it breaks down: a letter is read once and put away. Data in use may sit in memory for hours, giving an attacker a much wider window.

What these failures look like in practice

The abstract definitions become much easier to apply once you have concrete failure shapes in mind.

A confidentiality failure is loud and immediate. A misconfigured cloud storage bucket exposes customer records; the data is intact and the service is running normally, but people who should not have seen the data have seen it. You cannot un-disclose information, which is what makes confidentiality failures irreversible in a way the other two often are not. Notification obligations, regulatory penalties, and reputational damage all follow from that irreversibility.

An integrity failure is quiet and corrosive. An attacker with database access modifies transaction amounts over several months. Nothing appears broken. Reports run, systems respond, and users notice nothing. The damage surfaces only when someone reconciles against an external source, and by then the organization faces a worse problem than the original modification: it cannot say which records are trustworthy. Restoring from backup does not help if you do not know when the corruption began.

An availability failure is obvious and urgent. Ransomware encrypts file shares, or a volumetric attack saturates the connection. Everyone knows immediately, executive attention is total, and the recovery path is usually well understood even when it is painful. Availability failures generate the most noise and, often, the least lasting harm - which is precisely why organizations over-invest in availability and under-invest in integrity.

[EXAM] When a scenario describes damage that was not noticed for a long time, integrity is almost always the intended answer. When it describes immediate disruption, think availability. When it describes disclosure, think confidentiality.

Non-repudiation

✍️ Think of it like…

A notarised signature versus a typed name

Anyone can type your name at the bottom of a document - that proves nothing, because anyone could have typed it. That is a plain hash: it confirms the text has not changed, but not who stood behind it.

A notarised signature is different. The notary verifies identity and stamps the document, so you cannot later claim someone else signed it. That is a digital signature: because signing requires a private key only you hold, a valid signature is evidence that you acted.

Non-repudiation means a party cannot credibly deny having performed an action. It is not part of the triad but is tested alongside it. The mechanism is the digital signature: because signing requires a private key that only one party holds, a valid signature is evidence that party acted.

Note the distinction the exam cares about. A hash provides integrity but not non-repudiation - anyone can compute a hash. A digital signature provides integrity and non-repudiation, because it binds the integrity check to a specific private key holder.

Authentication, Authorization, and Accounting

AAA answers three separate questions that are easy to conflate: who are you, what may you do, and what did you do. Exam questions often describe a failure and ask which of the three broke down.

The three stages in order

The ordering matters. Authorization without prior authentication is meaningless, and accounting without authentication cannot attribute actions to a person. A system that logs "a file was deleted" has accounting; a system that logs "user jsmith deleted the file" has accounting built on authentication.

Tracing an access attempt through AAA

Authenticating people versus authenticating systems

People authenticate with something they know, have, or are. Systems and services cannot do any of that - there is no human present to enter a password. Machines authenticate with certificates, API keys, or managed service identities.

This distinction drives a category of exam question about service accounts. When a scenario describes a scheduled task or an application connecting to a database, the correct answer usually involves certificate-based or managed identity authentication rather than a stored password, precisely because no human can respond to an MFA prompt at 3 a.m.

[WARNING] Service accounts with static passwords that never expire and hold broad privileges are one of the most common real-world findings in security assessments. They fail authentication hygiene and authorization least-privilege simultaneously.

✈️ Think of it like…

Getting on a flight

Authentication happens at check-in, where you show ID to prove you are who you claim. Authorization is your boarding pass: it does not just say you may fly, it says which flight, which seat, and whether you may enter the lounge. Accounting is the passenger manifest recording who actually boarded.

This makes the failure modes obvious. Being turned away at check-in with a bad ID is an authentication failure. Being waved through check-in but stopped at the lounge door is an authorization failure - the airline knows exactly who you are, you simply are not permitted there. And a manifest that lists only a seat number without a name is accounting that cannot attribute anything to a person, which is what a shared admin account gives you.

The three authentication factors

Authentication factors are conventionally grouped into three categories, and multifactor authentication means combining factors from different categories - not simply requiring two things.

FactorCategoryExamplesPrincipal weakness
Something you knowKnowledgePassword, PIN, security question Can be guessed, phished, or reused across sites
Something you havePossessionHardware token, phone, smart card Can be lost, stolen, or intercepted (SMS)
Something you areInherenceFingerprint, face, iris Cannot be changed once compromised

💳 Think of it like…

An ATM withdrawal

An ATM requires your card (something you have) and your PIN (something you know). That is genuine multifactor authentication: a pickpocket with your card cannot withdraw cash, and a shoulder-surfer with your PIN cannot either. An attacker needs both, acquired in two different ways.

Now imagine an ATM that asked for your PIN and then your mother's maiden name. Two prompts, but both are things you know - so a single successful phishing call captures both. That is why a password plus a security question is not multifactor. Adding your fingerprint would be, because it comes from a third category entirely.

The distinction matters for a specific class of exam question. A password plus a security question is not multifactor: both are knowledge factors, so a single successful phishing attempt captures both. A password plus a push notification to a registered phone is multifactor, because compromising the password does not put the phone in the attacker's hands.

Biometrics carry a distinctive risk worth understanding. A leaked password is an inconvenience because you change it. A leaked fingerprint template is permanent - you have nine replacements and then you are out. This is why biometrics are generally deployed as one factor among several, and typically as a convenient local unlock rather than a remote authentication mechanism.

You will also see location and behaviour described as supplementary signals. These are not usually counted as true factors on their own, but they feed the adaptive identity model you will meet in the zero trust section: the same credentials presented from an unexpected place produce a weaker trust signal.

Why accounting is the stage that gets cut

Accounting is the least glamorous of the three and the first to be under-resourced. Logging costs storage, generates noise, and produces no visible benefit until something goes wrong. The result is a predictable pattern in incident response: an organization can confirm that a breach happened but cannot establish what the attacker touched.

Two properties separate useful accounting from box-ticking. Logs must be attributable, meaning tied to an authenticated identity rather than a shared account or an IP address alone. And they must be tamper-evident, because an attacker with administrative access will edit logs to cover their tracks. Forwarding logs to a separate system that the compromised host cannot write to is the standard answer, and it is why centralized logging appears in Domain 4 as an architectural requirement rather than an operational nicety.

Shared accounts break attribution completely. If five administrators use one admin login, accounting can tell you that admin deleted the records but not which human did it. That is why scenarios describing shared credentials are usually testing accountability rather than password strength.

Gap analysis

A gap analysis compares the current security state against a target state - a framework, a policy, or a regulatory requirement - and documents the difference. The output is a prioritized list of gaps, not a pass/fail verdict. Gap analysis appears in objective 1.1 because it is the process that connects the abstract concepts in this module to a concrete remediation plan.

Security Control Categories and Types

This is the highest-yield content in the module. CompTIA classifies every control along two independent axes, and questions routinely give you a control and ask for its position on one axis while offering distractors from the other.

Axis 1 - Category: who or what implements it

CategoryImplemented byExamples
TechnicalTechnologyFirewall, encryption, MFA, antivirus
ManagerialPolicy and planningRisk assessments, security policy, SDLC review
OperationalPeople executing proceduresSecurity awareness training, guard rotation, incident response
PhysicalTangible barriersFences, locks, badge readers, bollards, lighting

The distinction students most often miss is operational versus managerial. Managerial controls are about deciding and documenting; operational controls are about people doing the work day to day. Writing the incident response plan is managerial. Running the incident response is operational.

🏡 Think of it like…

Protecting your home

Every control type has a household equivalent. The deadbolt is preventive - it stops entry. The alarm company sign on the lawn is deterrent - it changes the burglar's mind before they try. The camera is detective - it tells you what happened. Insurance is corrective - it restores you afterwards. And the house rule that the last person out locks up is directive - it instructs behaviour.

Compensating controls fit too. If you live in a listed building and are not permitted to fit a modern deadbolt, you add a door chain, a camera, and a motion light instead. The required control is unavailable, so you assemble a substitute that reduces the risk comparably. That is precisely the unpatchable-legacy-system scenario in professional dress.

Where it breaks down: your house has one owner making decisions. In an organization, the person choosing the control, the person installing it, and the person living with the inconvenience are usually three different people - which is why change management exists.

Axis 2 - Type: what it does about the threat

TypeFunctionTiming
PreventiveStops the event from occurringBefore
DeterrentDiscourages the attemptBefore
DetectiveIdentifies that it occurredDuring or after
CorrectiveFixes damage and restores stateAfter
CompensatingSubstitutes when the primary control is not feasibleAny
DirectiveInstructs or mandates behaviorBefore

The two axes are independent

Any category can pair with any type. This is the source of most confusion, so work through the same control across types:

Nothing about the camera changed. The type changed with what the scenario says the camera accomplishes. When an exam question offers two types for the same device, reread the scenario for the outcome.

[EXAM] Deterrent and preventive are the most commonly confused pair. A deterrent affects an attacker's decision to try. A preventive control stops them whether or not they try. A warning banner is deterrent; a locked door is preventive.

Defence in depth

No single control is trusted to hold. Defence in depth layers controls so that the failure of any one does not produce a breach, and the layers are deliberately drawn from different categories - a technical control and a physical control fail for different reasons, so they are unlikely to fail simultaneously.

🏰 Think of it like…

A medieval castle

A castle never relied on one wall. An attacker faced a moat, then an outer wall, then a gate with a portcullis, then guards in the courtyard, and only then the keep. Each layer was independent: a ladder that defeated the wall did nothing about the moat, and bribing a guard did not open the portcullis.

That independence is the point of defence in depth, and it is why the layers are drawn from different control categories. A technical control and a physical control fail for different reasons, so they are unlikely to fail on the same day.

Consider protecting a database of customer records. A single control would be a password on the database. Layered, the same asset might be protected by network segmentation restricting which hosts can reach it, a firewall rule limiting the ports, authentication requiring valid credentials, authorization limiting which tables an account can read, encryption at rest so stolen disks are useless, monitoring alerting on unusual query volume, and backups enabling recovery. An attacker must defeat each layer in turn, and the monitoring layer means the attempt is likely to be noticed partway through.

🏨 Think of it like…

A hotel keycard

Your keycard opens your room, the gym, and the pool. It does not open other guests' rooms, the manager's office, or the safe behind reception. Housekeeping's card opens every guest room but not the office. The manager's opens nearly everything.

Nobody is being distrusted - each card simply carries the access its job requires. And when a card is lost, the damage is bounded by what that card could open. Least privilege does not stop the card being stolen; it decides how bad the theft is.

The related principle is least privilege: every account gets the minimum access needed for its function, and nothing more. Least privilege limits blast radius. It does not prevent compromise, but it determines how much a compromise is worth. A helpdesk account that can reset passwords is damaging; a helpdesk account with domain administrator rights is catastrophic, and the difference is a configuration decision rather than an attacker capability.

Choosing a control: the reasoning the exam expects

Scenario questions rarely ask which control is strongest. They ask which is most appropriate given a stated constraint, and the constraint is the key to the answer. Work in this order:

StepQuestion to ask
1. Identify the assetWhat is actually being protected, and how much is it worth?
2. Identify the threatWhat specifically goes wrong, and who causes it?
3. Read the constraintBudget, uptime, legacy system, regulation, timeline - there is always one
4. Match type to timingStop it, discourage it, notice it, or recover from it?
5. Check feasibilityIf the obvious control is blocked by the constraint, the answer is compensating

Step three is where most wrong answers come from. A scenario that mentions a system which "cannot be taken offline" or software that "the vendor no longer supports" has told you that the textbook answer is unavailable, and any option proposing it is a distractor no matter how technically sound it sounds.

Compensating controls

A compensating control is used when the required control cannot be implemented. Scenarios describing legacy systems that cannot be patched are almost always leading to a compensating control answer: the system cannot be patched, so it is isolated on a segmented network with restricted access and enhanced monitoring.

Note the exam's framing: compensating controls are a legitimate, documented response to a constraint, not a failure. What makes them acceptable is that they are chosen deliberately and provide comparable risk reduction.

Zero Trust and Physical Security

Why the perimeter model failed

Traditional network security assumed a trusted inside and an untrusted outside, separated by a firewall. Once authenticated at the perimeter, a user or device was broadly trusted. Cloud services, remote work, and mobile devices dissolved the perimeter, and the model's core weakness became decisive: an attacker who gets inside once inherits that broad trust and moves laterally without further challenge.

The zero trust principle

Zero trust removes implicit trust based on network location. Every request is authenticated, authorized, and evaluated against policy regardless of where it originates. The working summary is never trust, always verify - trust is never inherited from a prior decision or a network position.

🏢 Think of it like…

Badging into an office building

The old perimeter model is the lobby turnstile. You badge in once at the front door, and from then on you can walk into any office, any meeting room, and the server closet, because being inside the building is the authorisation. Anyone who tailgates through the turnstile inherits exactly the same freedom.

Zero trust replaces this with a badge reader on every interior door. Your badge is checked at each one, every time, and the system asks more than whether the badge is valid: is this person normally on this floor, at this hour, and is their access still current? A single tailgater now reaches the lobby and nowhere else.

Where it breaks down: a building has a fixed set of doors. A network has services appearing and disappearing constantly, which is why zero trust needs an accurate inventory before it can enforce anything.

Control plane and data plane

CompTIA splits the zero trust architecture into two planes, and the objective names specific components you should recognize.

The mental model: the control plane decides, the data plane enforces. The policy enforcement point sits in the traffic path and asks the control plane whether to allow each request.

[NOTE] Adaptive identity means the confidence in an identity changes with context - the same credentials from an unrecognized device in an unusual location produce a weaker trust signal and may trigger additional verification.

Microsegmentation and the end of the flat network

The practical expression of zero trust inside a network is microsegmentation: dividing the environment into small zones with policy enforced between them, rather than maintaining one large trusted interior. On a flat network, an attacker who compromises a receptionist's workstation can reach the finance server because both sit inside the perimeter. With microsegmentation, that workstation can reach only the specific services its role requires, and an attempt to reach anything else is both blocked and logged.

🚢 Think of it like…

Watertight compartments in a ship

Ships are built with sealed bulkheads dividing the hull into compartments. A breach floods the compartment it happened in - not the whole vessel. The ship survives damage that would sink an open hull.

Microsegmentation is the same design applied to a network. A compromised receptionist's workstation floods one compartment. Without it, the network is an open hull and a single leak reaches the engine room.

This directly attacks lateral movement, which is the phase where most breaches escalate from a single compromised endpoint into an organization-wide incident. The initial compromise is often unremarkable - a phishing click, a reused password. What turns it into a breach is the attacker's ability to move sideways toward something valuable.

Why zero trust migrations are difficult

Exam questions increasingly describe organizations partway through a zero trust migration, so it helps to understand why the destination is rarely reached cleanly.

The first obstacle is inventory. You cannot write policy for traffic you do not know exists. Most organizations discover during migration that they have undocumented dependencies - a reporting server that quietly reaches into a production database at 3 a.m., a legacy integration nobody remembers building. Enforcing policy before mapping these breaks production, which is why zero trust rollouts typically begin in a monitor-only mode that logs what would have been blocked.

The second is legacy systems. Zero trust assumes every component can participate in authentication and policy evaluation. Older systems often cannot: they support no modern authentication, cannot present device posture, and break when placed behind a proxy. These become the compensating-control cases from the previous section - isolated, restricted, and monitored rather than fully integrated.

The third is cultural. Zero trust removes standing access that people have relied on for years, and every removal is felt as friction by someone with a legitimate job to do. Migrations that ignore this produce shadow IT as staff route around the controls, which leaves the organization less secure than before it started.

[NOTE] Zero trust is an architecture and a strategy, not a product. Vendors sell components that support it, but no purchase makes an organization zero trust. Any exam option describing a single product as the complete answer is a distractor.

Deception and disruption technology

These controls deliberately present false targets to attackers.

TechnologyWhat it is
HoneypotA decoy system that appears to be a legitimate target
HoneynetA network of honeypots simulating a full environment
HoneyfileA decoy file that triggers an alert when accessed
HoneytokenFake credentials or data whose use signals a breach

🗺️ Think of it like…

Trap streets on a map

Cartographers historically inserted a fictitious street into their maps. The street served no navigational purpose - it existed solely so that if it appeared on a competitor's map, the copying was proven. No honest mapmaker could have surveyed a road that was never built.

A honeytoken works identically. Fake credentials sitting in a config file serve no legitimate purpose, so any use of them is proof of compromise. This is why deception controls produce almost no false positives while most detective controls drown analysts in noise.

Their shared value is detection quality. There is no legitimate reason to access a honeyfile, so an access is a high-confidence indicator of compromise with almost no false positives - unlike most detective controls, which drown analysts in noise.

Change Management

Change management gets its own objective (1.3) because uncontrolled change is a leading cause of both outages and security gaps. A firewall rule added at 2 a.m. to resolve an incident, never documented and never removed, is a permanent hole nobody knows exists.

🔨 Think of it like…

Renovating a house

You do not knock down a wall because it is in your way. You first check whether it is load-bearing - that is impact analysis. You pull a permit rather than deciding alone, because the consequences land on other people - that is approval. You schedule the noisy work when the household can tolerate it - a maintenance window. And when you are finished you update the blueprints, so the next person who opens that wall knows what is behind it.

Skipping the last step is how houses end up with wiring nobody can explain and pipes in unexpected places. Skipping it in a network is how an organization ends up with a firewall rule nobody can account for - which is exactly the scenario in Lab 3.

Who approves, and why a board exists

The change advisory board exists to bring the people who will be affected into the decision before it is made. A typical board includes representation from operations, security, the application owner, and the business unit that depends on the service. The composition is the point: an engineer evaluating their own change will assess technical risk accurately and business risk poorly, because they do not know that Thursday is payroll processing.

⚕️ Think of it like…

A surgical team briefing

Before an operation the team pauses to confirm the patient, the procedure, and the site aloud. The surgeon already knows all of it. The briefing exists because the surgeon is not the only person whose knowledge matters, and because the one time assumptions diverge is the time it matters most.

A change advisory board serves the same function. The engineer understands the technical risk better than anyone in the room. What they do not know is that Thursday is payroll processing, or that another team's release depends on the same component.

Boards also standardise the treatment of routine work. Most mature processes define pre-approved standard changes - low-risk, well-understood, repeatedly executed - that proceed without individual review. This matters because a process that requires a board meeting to patch a workstation will be bypassed within a month, and a bypassed process protects nothing. Right-sizing the approval burden is what keeps the process alive.

What a change can break

The objective lists technical implications, but they are easier to remember as a set of questions to ask before implementing.

ImplicationQuestion to ask beforehand
Allow and deny listsDoes this change an address, port, or certificate referenced elsewhere?
Restricted activitiesIs any part of this out of scope for the approved window?
DowntimeWho cannot work while this runs, and have they been told?
Service restartWill dependent services reconnect on their own, or need manual intervention?
Application restartAre in-flight transactions lost, and does that matter?
Legacy applicationsIs anything here unsupported and likely to fail in unexpected ways?
DependenciesWhat else consumes this component, and was it tested?

Dependencies cause the most incidents because they are the hardest to enumerate. A certificate renewal that looks like a single-server change can take down six services that pinned that certificate, none of which appeared in the change record. This is why impact analysis is a distinct stage rather than a paragraph in the request.

Documentation and version control

Documentation and version control

Two outputs must survive every change: updated diagrams and updated policies or procedures. A network diagram that no longer matches reality actively causes harm during an incident, when responders trust it and act on stale information.

Version control tracks changes over time so any modification can be identified, attributed, and reverted. Applied to infrastructure as code and configuration files, it makes the change history itself an audit trail - which is why version control appears in a security exam rather than only a development one.

Version control also underpins the ability to answer the question an auditor actually asks, which is not "is this configuration correct" but "who changed it, when, and who approved it." A configuration file in version control answers all three from the commit history. The same file edited directly on a production host answers none of them.

[EXAM] Emergency changes are the exception the exam likes to probe. An urgent fix may legitimately bypass advance approval, but it never bypasses documentation. The expected answer is retroactive review: the change is made, then submitted to the change advisory board after the fact so the record is complete and the temporary fix does not become permanent.
[OK] A useful test for whether change management is working: pick a production firewall rule at random and ask who requested it, when, and why. If nobody can answer, the process exists on paper only.

[BEFORE YOU SUBMIT] Answers are stored in this browser only. If you clear browsing data, use a different computer, or work in private browsing, they will not follow you. Generate the report and save the file when you finish a lab.
