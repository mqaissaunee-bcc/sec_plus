Identity, Automation, Incident Response, and Forensics

Managing identity from provisioning to attestation, access control models and privileged access, automation and its costs, the incident response process, and handling digital evidence so it remains usable.

Progress: 0% complete

Overview

[OBJECTIVES] By the end of this module you will be able to manage accounts from provisioning through attestation to deprovisioning; compare the access control models and select one for a scenario; apply MFA, password, and privileged access controls; identify where automation helps and where it adds risk; work the incident response process in the correct order; and handle digital evidence so it remains usable.

This module carries four objectives and completes Domain 4. It is the densest in the course, and the four topics are genuinely connected: identity decides who can act, automation decides what happens without a human, incident response is what you do when those controls fail, and forensics is how you establish what actually happened.

[STUDY NOTE] This module covers more ground than the others. If you are working through it in one sitting, the natural break is after automation - the first half is preventive controls, the second half is what happens after something goes wrong.

The thread connecting the four

Identity is the control that matters most in practice, because nearly every modern breach involves credentials rather than exploits. Automation determines whether identity controls are applied consistently or depend on someone remembering. Incident response assumes both will eventually fail. And forensics determines whether you can prove what happened afterwards.

The connection is worth holding because exam scenarios move between them. A question about an account that stayed active after an employee left is an identity question, an automation question, and - once it is used - an incident response question.

Identity Management

Provisioning and deprovisioning

Provisioning creates an account with the access a role requires. Deprovisioning removes it when the person leaves or changes role. The second is where organizations fail, because nothing stops working when it is skipped.

A missed deprovisioning produces an orphaned account: valid credentials with no owner and nobody monitoring them. Module 3 established why this matters - a former employee with retained access bypasses the perimeter legitimately, and the account's activity looks like authorised use.

The related failure is privilege creep. Someone moves from finance to operations to procurement over eight years, gaining access at each step and losing none, and ends up with a combination of permissions no role was ever designed to hold. Role changes require removing old access as well as granting new, and that half is routinely omitted because nobody complains about access they still have.

🗝 Think of it like…

Keys issued and never handed back

Every new starter is issued keys for the doors their job needs. Nobody objects, because they need them. When someone changes department they are issued the new set - and asking for the old ones back is an awkward conversation nobody schedules.

After a decade, long-serving staff carry keys to nearly every door in the building, and departed staff have simply walked away with theirs. No single decision caused this. It is the accumulated result of the returning half of the process being the half nobody is accountable for.

What deprovisioning misses

Identity proofing and attestation

Identity proofing establishes that a person is who they claim before an account is created. Everything downstream depends on it: strong authentication protecting an account issued to the wrong person protects the wrong person very well.

Attestation is the periodic review in which managers and system owners confirm that each person's access is still appropriate. It is the control that catches privilege creep and orphaned accounts, and its characteristic failure is rubber-stamping - a manager presented with two hundred entries and a deadline approves them all.

Attestation works when it is scoped small enough to be read, when the reviewer is someone who actually knows what the person does, and when removing access is at least as easy as approving it.

🏦 Think of it like…

Opening an account at a bank

Before a bank issues you anything, it asks for documents and checks them. Everything afterwards - the card, the app, the fraud alerts - rests entirely on that first conversation being correct.

If the documents were forged and the account was opened in your name for someone else, the bank's excellent card security now protects a stranger very effectively. Identity proofing is that first conversation, and no amount of authentication strength compensates for getting it wrong.

Single sign-on and federation

Single sign-on allows one authentication to grant access to multiple systems. Federation extends this across organizational boundaries, so an identity issued by one organization is accepted by another.

The security argument runs both ways, and the exam expects both. SSO reduces password reuse and fatigue, gives one place to enforce MFA, and makes deprovisioning genuinely effective - disable the identity and access ends everywhere. It also concentrates risk: compromising that single identity opens everything at once, which is why SSO accounts require strong MFA rather than merely benefiting from it.

ProtocolPurposeTypical use
SAMLAuthentication - asserting who someone isEnterprise web SSO
OAuthAuthorisation - delegating access without sharing credentialsThird-party app access to your data
OpenID ConnectAuthentication layer built on OAuthConsumer sign-in with an existing account
LDAPDirectory access protocolQuerying an on-premises directory

The SAML and OAuth distinction is the one most often confused. SAML answers "who is this person"; OAuth answers "what may this application do on their behalf". An application granted OAuth access to your calendar has not been told who you are in any useful sense - it has been given permission to read calendar entries.

🚗 Think of it like…

A passport and a valet key

SAML is a passport. It asserts identity, is issued by an authority the other party already trusts, and the border officer accepts it because of who issued it rather than because they know you.

OAuth is a valet key. It opens the driver's door and starts the engine and does not open the boot or the glovebox. It grants a specific, limited capability - and the attendant never learns your name, because that was never the point.

Where it breaks down: a valet key stops working when you drive away. An OAuth grant persists until it is revoked, which is why reviewing which applications hold access to your accounts is a real control rather than housekeeping.

Interoperability

Interoperability is whether identity systems can actually work together. It appears in the objective because federation frequently fails for unglamorous reasons: systems supporting different protocols, attributes named differently on each side, or legacy applications that cannot participate in modern authentication at all.

Those legacy applications are the practical problem. An organization deploying SSO across ninety percent of its estate still has local accounts on the remaining ten percent - and those are the accounts that survive deprovisioning, because disabling the central identity does not touch them.

Access Control, MFA, and Privileged Access

The access control models

ModelWho decidesCharacteristic use
Mandatory (MAC)The system, from labels; users cannot overrideMilitary and classified environments
Discretionary (DAC)The data owner grants access at their discretionFile shares, personal documents
Role-based (RBAC)Access follows job roleMost enterprises
Rule-basedSystem-wide rules applied uniformlyFirewall and network policy
Attribute-based (ABAC)Policy evaluating multiple attributes togetherFine-grained and contextual access

Two distinctions decide most exam questions. MAC versus DAC is about whether a user can share what they have access to: under DAC they can, under MAC the system forbids it regardless of their wishes. And RBAC versus ABAC is about how many factors the decision considers - RBAC looks only at role, while ABAC can combine role, device, location, time, and data classification in one policy.

ABAC is what makes the zero trust decisions from Module 1 expressible. "Permit finance staff to read payroll from a managed device during business hours from a recognised location" is a single ABAC policy and cannot be written in RBAC at all.

👨 Think of it like…

Four doorkeepers

The DAC doorkeeper works for whoever owns the room: the owner says who may enter and can lend their key to a friend.

The MAC doorkeeper works for the institution. The room is marked Secret, your clearance is Confidential, and the owner's opinion is irrelevant - they cannot let you in even if they want to.

The RBAC doorkeeper reads your job title. Radiologists enter, everyone else does not, and no further conversation occurs.

The ABAC doorkeeper checks your title, whether you are on shift, which building you came from, and whether the room is currently in restricted use. Same person, different answer on a different day.

Least privilege and time-of-day restrictions

Least privilege was introduced in Module 1 and is implemented here. Permission assignments should grant the minimum an account needs, and the implications run further than students expect: permissions granted to a service account become the permissions available to anything that compromises that service.

Time-of-day restrictions limit when an account may be used. They are cheap and surprisingly effective, because they convert the out-of-cycle indicator from Module 5 from something you detect afterwards into something that simply does not happen.

Multifactor authentication

Module 1 established the three factor categories, and this objective adds a fourth - somewhere you are, based on location - and names specific implementations.

ImplementationFactorNotes
BiometricsSomething you areCannot be changed once compromised
Hard tokenSomething you haveDedicated device generating codes
Soft tokenSomething you haveApplication on a phone
Security keySomething you haveHardware key; resistant to phishing
SMS codeSomething you haveWeakest common option - interceptable, SIM swap

Security keys deserve attention because they solve a problem other methods do not. A user can be phished into typing a code from an authenticator app into an attacker's page. A security key verifies the site's identity cryptographically before responding, so it will not authenticate to a lookalike domain - which is the typosquatting attack from Module 3 defeated by the mechanism rather than by the user noticing.

🔐 Think of it like…

A safe needing two different keys

A safe deposit box opens only when the customer's key and the bank's key are turned together. Neither party can open it alone, and that is deliberate - the security comes from the two keys being held by different people in different places.

Multifactor authentication is the same design. The point is not two obstacles but two obstacles an attacker must overcome in two different ways. A password plus a security question is one key turned twice.

Password concepts

Guidance has shifted, and the exam reflects current thinking rather than older habits.

ConceptCurrent guidance
LengthThe dominant factor; longer beats complex
ComplexityUseful, but composition rules produce predictable substitutions
ReuseProhibited - reuse is what makes credential stuffing work
ExpirationForced periodic change is discouraged unless compromise is suspected
AgeMinimum age prevents cycling straight back to a favourite
Password managersRecommended; make long unique passwords practical
PasswordlessRemoves the password entirely, using keys or biometrics

The expiration change surprises students. Forced ninety-day rotation was standard for years and is now discouraged, because it produces predictable behaviour - Summer2026 becomes Autumn2026 - without meaningfully limiting an attacker who exploits credentials within hours. Change on suspicion of compromise, not on a calendar.

Privileged access management

Administrative accounts are the highest-value targets in any environment, and PAM tooling exists to reduce how much standing privilege exists at all.

TechniqueWhat it does
Just-in-time permissionsElevation granted for a specific task and window, then removed automatically
Password vaultingCredentials held in a vault, checked out and rotated after use
Ephemeral credentialsCredentials created for one session and never reused

The shared principle is that privilege should not sit waiting to be stolen. An administrator holding permanent domain admin rights is a permanent target; an administrator who requests elevation for twenty minutes with an approval trail is a target for twenty minutes.

⏳ Think of it like…

A key that expires at four o'clock

A contractor needing access to the plant room could be given a master key to keep. From that moment the key exists in a pocket, at home, in a car, and in a coat left in a pub - and it opens the plant room for years.

Alternatively they collect a key that stops working at four, signed out against their name. The work is identical. What differs is that the window in which the key is worth stealing is three hours rather than the rest of its existence.

Permission assignment in practice

Objective 4.6 mentions permission assignments and their implications, and two implications recur in exam scenarios.

Group nesting. Permissions are granted to groups rather than individuals so they scale, and groups are then placed inside other groups for convenience. Several layers in, nobody can state what a given group actually confers without tracing the chain, and someone added to a helpful-sounding group inherits far more than anyone intended. Attestation is the control that surfaces this, which is why the review must show effective permissions rather than group names.

Service account permissions. An account used by an application holds whatever that application was granted, and it typically has no password expiry, no MFA, and no human watching it. Whatever compromises the application inherits those permissions in full. Service accounts should be scoped tightly, and their credentials belong in a vault rather than a configuration file - which is the Module 2 key management failure repeated with a different kind of secret.

Automation and Orchestration

Where automation is applied

Objective 4.7 names use cases, and they cluster into work that is repetitive, must be consistent, or must happen faster than a human can respond.

Use caseWhat automation delivers
User provisioning and deprovisioningAccess granted and removed reliably, on the same day
Resource provisioningInfrastructure built to a defined standard every time
Guard railsPolicy that prevents non-compliant configurations being created
Security groupsMembership derived from authoritative data rather than requests
Ticket creation and escalationFindings routed to owners without manual triage
Enabling and disabling servicesRapid response actions applied consistently
Continuous integration and testingSecurity checks run on every change
Integrations and APIsTools acting on each other's findings

Deprovisioning is the highest-value item on that list. It is the control that fails most often when performed manually, and it fails silently - which makes it exactly the kind of work automation is for. Connecting the identity system to the HR system means a leaving date entered once removes access everywhere, without depending on a manager remembering to raise a ticket.

Guard rails deserve separate mention because they change when enforcement happens. Rather than detecting a public storage container after it is created, a guard rail prevents it being created. That is the difference between finding a problem and not having one.

🔨 Think of it like…

A jig on a workbench

A skilled carpenter can cut the same joint by hand a hundred times, and the hundred will vary slightly - more so at the end of a long day. A jig holds the work so the cut can only be made one way. It does not make the carpenter better; it makes the wrong cut difficult.

That is what a guard rail does. It is not a check performed after the work, it is a constraint that makes the incorrect outcome hard to produce in the first place.

💳 Think of it like…

Borrowing time you will repay with interest

A quick script written in an afternoon saves hours every week from then on. The saving is real and immediate, and the cost arrives later - when it becomes load-bearing, when the person who wrote it leaves, and when an API it depends on changes silently.

That is technical debt. Like financial debt it is not inherently wrong, and borrowing deliberately with a plan to repay is different from borrowing without noticing you have.

The benefits, stated precisely

BenefitWhat it means
Efficiency and time savingStaff time moves from repetition to judgement
Enforcing baselinesThe Module 8 baseline becomes the actual configuration, not a document
Standard configurationsSystems built identically, so drift has less opportunity
Scaling securelyTen thousand endpoints handled as consistently as ten
Employee retentionAnalysts leave roles that consist of repetitive manual work
Reaction timeContainment in seconds rather than after a triage queue
Workforce multiplierA small team covers ground that would otherwise need a large one

Employee retention appears in the objective and looks out of place until you consider what security operations work is actually like. Roles consisting largely of closing repetitive alerts have high turnover, and every departure costs institutional knowledge. Automating the repetitive portion is a retention measure with a direct security consequence.

What automation costs

ConsiderationThe risk
ComplexitySystems nobody fully understands behave unpredictably
CostTooling and the expertise to build and maintain it
Single point of failureIf the automation platform fails, everything it does stops
Technical debtQuick scripts become critical infrastructure nobody wants to touch
Ongoing supportabilityAutomation written by someone who left is a liability

Supportability is the practical trap. An analyst writes a script that saves hours weekly, it becomes load-bearing, and they leave. Nobody else understands it, nobody dares change it, and it silently stops working the day an API it depends on changes. Automation is code, so it needs version control, documentation, and an owner - which is the Module 8 asset ownership principle applied to something that is not a device.

Automation also amplifies mistakes. A manual error affects one system; the same error in automation affects every system it touches, at machine speed. This is the infrastructure-as-code trade-off from Module 6 in a broader form, and it is why automation that makes changes needs the same review as any other production code.

Incident Response

The process

Objective 4.8 names seven phases, and the exam tests both their content and their order.

PhaseWhat happens
PreparationPlans, tooling, training, and contacts established before anything happens
DetectionSomething is noticed - by monitoring, a user, or a third party
AnalysisDetermine what happened, what is affected, and how serious it is
ContainmentStop it spreading or continuing
EradicationRemove the attacker and the cause
RecoveryRestore normal operation and confirm it is safe
Lessons learnedReview what happened and improve the response

Two orderings are tested repeatedly. Containment precedes eradication: stop the bleeding before treating the wound, because eradication takes time and the attacker is still active during it. And preparation precedes everything - a plan written during an incident is not a plan, it is improvisation with an audience.

💧 Think of it like…

A burst pipe at two in the morning

You do not begin by repairing the pipe. You find the stopcock and turn the water off, because the repair takes an hour and the flooding does not pause while you work.

Containment is the stopcock. Eradication is the repair. Recovery is drying out and turning the water back on, and lessons learned is asking why nobody knew where the stopcock was - which is preparation for next time.

Containment decisions

Containment involves a genuine trade-off the exam likes to probe. Isolating a compromised host stops the attacker and destroys volatile evidence if done by powering off. Leaving a system running preserves evidence and lets the attacker continue.

The standard resolution is network isolation rather than shutdown: disconnect the host from the network while leaving it running, which halts lateral movement and exfiltration while preserving memory contents. This is the quarantine capability from Module 9, and it is why EDR tooling that can isolate a host remotely is valuable beyond detection.

Training, testing, and root cause analysis

Module 7 covered the exercise types - tabletop, simulation, and full failover - in a continuity context. They apply identically to incident response, and the same pattern holds: organizations run tabletops repeatedly because they are safe, and discover during a real incident that nobody has actually performed the technical steps.

Root cause analysis asks why the incident was possible rather than what the attacker did. The distinction matters because the proximate cause is rarely the useful answer. "An employee clicked a phishing link" is what happened; "MFA was not enforced on that application, so a harvested password was sufficient" is why it mattered. Only the second suggests a fix.

Threat hunting is proactive rather than reactive. It assumes an intruder may already be present and searches for evidence without waiting for an alert - which is the correct response to the long-dwell-time scenarios from Module 3, where the whole problem is that nothing alerted for fourteen months.

Working an incident in order

Who is involved, and communication

Incident response is not solely a technical activity, and the preparation phase includes establishing who is involved before anyone needs them.

RoleWhy they are needed
Incident commanderOne person deciding, so parallel efforts do not conflict
Technical respondersAnalysis, containment, eradication, recovery
Legal counselNotification obligations, legal hold, evidence handling
CommunicationsWhat is said to staff, customers, and press, and when
Executive sponsorAuthority for decisions with business consequences
HRRequired from the first hour where an insider is involved

The incident commander role matters more than it appears. Without one, an incident produces several people independently deciding to isolate, rebuild, or preserve the same system - and the rebuild destroys the evidence the preservation was collecting.

Communication also has a security dimension. If an attacker still has access to corporate email or chat, discussing the response there tells them exactly what you know and what you are about to do. Out-of-band communication during an active incident is a standard precaution.

Lessons learned

Lessons learned

The final phase is the one most often skipped, because by the time it arrives everyone is exhausted and the immediate problem is solved. Skipping it means the same incident recurs.

A useful review is blameless, which is the same principle as the reporting channel from Module 3. A review that identifies who made a mistake produces defensiveness and incomplete information; a review that identifies which control was missing produces changes. The question is not who clicked the link but why clicking a link was sufficient to cause this.

Digital Forensics and Data Sources

Why forensic handling matters

Evidence that has been altered, or that cannot be shown to be unaltered, is not usable - in court, in an employment tribunal, or in an insurance claim. The technical work of an investigation and the evidential work run in parallel, and the second is easy to destroy while doing the first.

Chain of custody

Chain of custody is the documented record of who held evidence, when, and what they did with it, unbroken from collection to presentation. A gap anywhere allows the argument that evidence could have been altered, and that argument does not need to be proven to be effective.

Practically this means every transfer is recorded and signed, evidence is stored so access is controlled and logged, and analysis is performed on a copy while the original is preserved untouched.

💼 Think of it like…

Signatures on an evidence bag

An item collected at a scene goes into a sealed bag, and every person who takes it signs and dates the label. The chain is not there because anyone is suspected - it is there so that nobody can suggest an unaccounted-for gap.

A bag with a four-hour period nobody signed for is not proof that anything happened during those hours. It only has to raise the possibility, which is enough to make the contents worthless as evidence.

Acquisition and order of volatility

Acquisition is collecting the evidence, and the order of volatility matters because some evidence disappears faster than the rest. The principle is to collect the most volatile first.

OrderSourceSurvives
1CPU registers and cacheMoments
2Memory (RAM)Until power is lost
3Network state and connectionsUntil the session ends
4Running processesUntil reboot
5Disk contentsPersistent
6Logs on remote systemsUntil retention expires
7Backups and archivesLongest

This is why powering off a compromised machine is usually wrong. Everything in memory - running malware, encryption keys, active network connections, and processes with no file on disk at all - is lost the instant power is removed, and it is often the only place that evidence existed.

Acquisition itself must be verifiable. A forensic image is hashed immediately, and the hash is recorded and re-verified later, which demonstrates that the copy analysed is identical to what was collected - the Module 2 integrity property applied to evidence.

🐌 Think of it like…

Footprints and an incoming tide

An investigator on a beach photographs the footprints near the water first. The wall behind them will still be there tomorrow; the prints will not survive the next hour.

Order of volatility is exactly that judgement. The disk can wait. Memory cannot, and pulling the power is the equivalent of letting the tide in before anyone took a photograph.

Acquiring evidence in the right order

🗑 Think of it like…

Stopping the shredder

An office shreds routine paperwork every Friday, and this is good practice - it reduces what could be exposed and what must be stored. Then a dispute becomes likely, and that same Friday routine turns into destroying material someone is about to ask for.

A legal hold is the instruction that stops the shredder. The awkward part is that in most organizations the shredding is automated, so the question becomes whether anyone can actually pause it - and finding out for the first time under legal pressure is a bad moment to discover the answer.

Legal hold, preservation, and e-discovery

TermMeaning
Legal holdA formal instruction to preserve data relevant to anticipated litigation
PreservationThe practical steps taken to comply, including suspending deletion
E-discoveryThe process of identifying and producing electronic evidence for legal proceedings
ReportingDocumenting findings, methods, and conclusions so they can be reviewed

A legal hold overrides normal retention policy, and this is the point that appears in exam scenarios. Routine deletion that would otherwise be correct - and that Module 7 described as good practice - becomes destruction of evidence once a hold is in place. Automated retention policies must therefore be capable of being suspended, and an organization whose deletion cannot be paused has a serious problem the moment litigation is anticipated.

🧩 Think of it like…

Two witnesses whose accounts disagree

When two witnesses describe the same evening identically, you learn what happened. When they disagree, you learn something more interesting: at least one is wrong, and working out which is often the whole investigation.

Host logs and network logs are those witnesses. Agreement is unremarkable; disagreement is the finding. A connection the firewall recorded and the host has no memory of tells you the host cannot be trusted to describe itself.

Data sources for an investigation

Objective 4.9 names the sources, and each answers a different question.

SourceWhat it establishes
Firewall logsWhat connections were permitted or denied, and to where
Application logsWhat was done inside the application, and by which account
Endpoint logsProcess execution, file changes, local activity
OS security logsAuthentication, privilege use, account changes
IDS / IPS logsWhat matched a signature or deviated from baseline
Network logs and flow dataWho communicated with whom, and how much
MetadataFile and message properties - timestamps, authorship, routing
Packet capturesFull content, where it was captured and is not encrypted
Vulnerability scansWhat weakness was likely used
Dashboards and reportsAggregate view and trend

Investigations rely on correlating these rather than on any one. The rootkit indicator from Module 5 is a case in point: the finding was that network logs and host logs disagreed, and neither source alone said anything.

Metadata is the source most often overlooked and frequently decisive. Email headers establish routing that the message body conceals, and document properties establish authorship and timing that the visible content does not.

[EXAM] Three discriminators. Containment precedes eradication - stop the spread before removing the cause. Isolate a host from the network rather than powering it off, because memory is lost with power. And a legal hold overrides retention policy, so routine deletion becomes evidence destruction the moment one is issued.
