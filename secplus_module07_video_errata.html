Episode 1: Protecting What an Organization Holds

01_Data_Protection · 7:12

An overview episode. It introduces RPO and RTO and previews classification. Episode 2 covers most of the same classification material again in more depth, so expect repetition.

  1. 0:36
    Clarification

    Video:hitting zero data loss is technically impossible and incredibly expensive

    Module:Makes no claim that near-zero data loss is impossible. Replication, journaling, and continuous data protection exist precisely to push RPO very low. The real limit is cost and whether the business actually needs it, because RPO is a business tolerance, not a technical ceiling.
  2. 3:18
    Clarification

    Video:Maps the library rooms directly to levels: reference room … that’s private, rare books room … that’s restricted.

    Module:The library analogy has four areas (open shelves, reference room, rare books room, archive) and illustrates one idea: label once and the handling rules follow automatically. It is not a one-to-one map of CompTIA levels. Don’t memorize the rooms; memorize the six levels. The module also notes where the analogy breaks: a book stays in one room, but data gets copied everywhere, so the label must travel with the data.
  3. 4:39
    Correction

    Video:A simple three-tier scheme will always outperform a complex one.

    Module:“Three or four well-understood levels outperform a precise scheme nobody applies correctly.” Four levels is fine, and there is no “always.” Note too that an organization choosing to use fewer levels doesn’t excuse you from knowing all six CompTIA labels for the exam.

Episode 2: Data Types and Classification

02_Data_Types_Classification · 9:28

  1. 2:45slide
    Correction

    Video:The classification table lists the handling for Critical as High-availability.

    Module:Critical handling is “availability and integrity emphasis.” Critical data must stay present and correct; a price list that is online but wrong stops the business just as surely as one that has vanished.
  2. 2:41
    Missing

    The narration describes handling for Public and Restricted only, and the slide abbreviates the rest.

    Module:Know the typical handling for every level:
    • Public: no harm from disclosure; no restriction.
    • Private: internal, personal, or non-public; internal access only.
    • Sensitive: harm if disclosed; encryption and controlled access.
    • Confidential: significant harm if disclosed; strong access control and logging.
    • Restricted: severe harm, often regulated; need-to-know and strict audit.
    • Critical: essential to operations; availability and integrity emphasis.
  3. 7:21
    Missing

    Video:Says embedded labels let automated security tools do their jobs.

    Module:Names the tool: data loss prevention (DLP). Labels stored in file metadata, headers, or document properties survive copying, which is what lets DLP act on them. A label recorded only in a spreadsheet describes where the data was when someone last looked.

Episode 3: Protecting Data — States, Sovereignty, and Choosing the Right Method

03_Protecting_Data · 6:37

  1. 1:38
    Missing

    Video:Names TLS as the protection for data in transit.

    Module:Data in transit is protected by TLS or IPSec. Expect both on the exam.
  2. 3:02
    Missing

    Geographic restrictions are presented only as a compliance tool for where data is stored.

    Module:Geographic restrictions (sometimes called geofencing) also limit where data may be accessed from. That access side is a security control in its own right: blocking authentication from countries where the organization has no staff removes a large volume of attack traffic.
  3. 3:40slide
    Missing

    The “decision engine” table shows only three of the eight methods: encryption, hashing, and tokenization. Masking appears only inside a scenario.

    Module:The full selection table also includes:
    • Masking: usually not recoverable in that view; choose it when partial visibility suffices, such as support screens or test data.
    • Obfuscation: recoverability varies; raises attacker effort but is never a primary control.
    • Segmentation: limits which systems can reach the data at all.
    • Permission restrictions: limit which people can reach it.
    • Geographic restrictions: sovereignty, or reducing access attack surface.
    The first five are transformations applied to the data; the last three are restrictions on reaching it. A complete strategy uses both, which is defence in depth applied to data.
  4. 6:20
    Clarification

    Video:Closes with an open question: if a foreign government seizes the server, whose laws are really protecting your secrets?

    Module:The module answers this directly. Data is subject to the laws of the country where it is physically stored, including that country’s laws about government access, regardless of who owns it. Encryption may keep the contents unreadable, but it does not change which jurisdiction applies. That is why storage location is a compliance question before it is a technical one.

Episode 4: High Availability — Keeping Systems Running

04_High_Availability · 6:58

  1. 1:40
    Missing

    The video explains that cluster nodes share state but never says what load-balanced nodes do.

    Module:Load-balanced nodes are often independent; cluster nodes coordinate and share state. This is one of the four rows the module uses to compare them (purpose, nodes, primary gain, state).
  2. 2:05slide too
    Clarification

    Video:Load balancing is like a manager opening more tills to process the lines faster.

    Module:Load balancing is the person directing shoppers to whichever till is shortest; every till is already open. Distributing work across existing active nodes is load balancing. Adding nodes is scaling out, which is a different idea.
  3. 4:15
    Missing

    Multi-cloud is justified only as protection against a provider outage.

    Module:Multi-cloud also protects against a commercial dispute with a provider, reduces vendor lock-in, and improves negotiating position. The module also warns that multi-cloud adopted for resilience alone frequently produces more incidents than it prevents.
  4. 4:56slide
    Clarification

    Video:Capacity planning slide: Infrastructure: Enough space and power?

    Module:Infrastructure covers power, cooling, space, and connectivity. Technology covers compute, storage, and bandwidth. For people, the module names three controls: cross-training, documentation, and a defined on-call rotation. The video mentions only the first two.
  5. 5:53slide
    Clarification

    Video:Generator carries the load for hours.

    Module:A generator covers extended outages for hours to days, while fuel lasts.
  6. 6:03
    Missing

    The UPS is described only as bridging the gap until the generator starts.

    Module:The UPS has a second job: if the generator fails to start, the UPS carries the load across a graceful shutdown so systems don’t simply crash.

Episode 5: Backups and Recovery — Making Sure You Can Get It Back

05_Backups_and_Recovery · 7:57

  1. 1:00
    Missing

    The video never discusses backup encryption as a consideration in its own right. Encryption keys come up only as something that gets lost.

    Module:Encryption is one of the seven backup considerations named in objective 3.4. It decides whether stolen backup media is a breach. An unencrypted backup tape that goes missing is a data breach even if production was never touched.
  2. 3:28
    Missing

    Journaling and continuous data protection are not mentioned anywhere in the series.

    Module:Journaling keeps a record of every change, allowing precise point-in-time recovery. Together with continuous data protection, it serves very low RPO requirements: you can restore to the moment immediately before a corrupting transaction rather than to last midnight. Journaling is named in the objective; know it.
  3. 3:50slide
    Clarification

    Video:3-2-1 slide lists 2 different media types, 1 held offsite, 1 immutable copy.

    Module:The rule is 3 copies (production plus two backups), on 2 media, with 1 offsite. The ransomware-era addition is one copy that is immutable or offline. The narration says this correctly; the slide drops the “3 copies” line and the “or offline” option.
  4. 5:05slide
    Correction

    Video:Backup types table says an incremental backup copies Any changes.

    Module:An incremental copies the changes since the last backup of any kind (full or incremental). A differential copies the changes since the last full backup. “Any changes” blurs exactly the distinction the exam tests. The narration gets this right.

Episode 6: Continuity, Recovery Sites, and Testing the Plan

06_Recovery_Continuity · 8:24

  1. 1:35slide
    Clarification

    Video:Site table describes a cold site as Space Only.

    Module:A cold site provides space, power, and connectivity, but no equipment or data. The narration says this correctly.
  2. 3:37
    Missing

    Geographic dispersion is explained, but without a rule of thumb for distance.

    Module:“Same city” is usually too close for anything but a building-level failure. Distance adds latency, which limits synchronous replication, so the right distance is far enough to escape a shared regional event and close enough to remain workable.
  3. 3:54slide
    Correction

    Video:Section title reads Continuity Operations.

    Module:The term is continuity of operations (often abbreviated COOP).
  4. 3:58
    Missing

    Continuity of operations is presented only as manual workarounds.

    Module:Continuity is broader than disaster recovery. DR restores technology; a continuity of operations plan covers people, premises, suppliers, and manual workarounds. The manual workaround is the element most often missing, which is why the video emphasizes it, but it is not the whole plan.
  5. 5:34
    Missing

    The four test types are described, but not what each one is good at finding or what the exam rewards.

    Module:A tabletop reveals people and process gaps: nobody knows who declares a disaster, or the plan calls a supplier who is no longer contracted. Only a real failover reveals that the recovery site’s certificate expired, its firewall rules were never updated, or its capacity is half what production needs. Organizations tend to repeat tabletops and never run a failover, so the exam treats scheduling a full failover as the mature answer.
  6. 6:14slide
    Missing

    The requirement-mapping table shows four of the module’s seven rows, and trims the controls in the rows it does show.

    Module:The complete map:
    • Low RPO: frequent backups, replication, journaling.
    • Low RTO: hot site, tested failover, standby capacity.
    • Survive site loss: offsite backups, geographic dispersion.
    • Survive ransomware: immutable or offline copies, segmentation.
    • Survive vendor failure: platform diversity, multi-cloud. (not in video)
    • Survive staff absence: cross-training, documentation, on-call rotation. (not in video)
    • Survive power loss: UPS for the gap, generator for duration, both tested. (not in video)
  7. 7:32
    Correction

    Video:Episodes one through three were entirely about confidentiality and integrity.

    Module:Data protection is largely about confidentiality and integrity, not entirely. Episode 1 opened with RPO and RTO, which are availability measures, and the Critical classification level is explicitly an availability judgement. The module’s point is that the two halves together complete the CIA triad.

Not covered anywhere in the series

These parts of the module have no counterpart in the videos. Read them on the module page.

  • The objective split. Objective 3.3 covers how data is classified and guarded; objective 3.4 covers how systems survive failure and how quickly they come back.
  • Cross-references to other modules. The protection mechanisms (encryption, hashing, tokenization, masking, obfuscation) and the three data states are explained in Module 2. RPO and RTO are formally defined alongside business impact analysis in Module 11. Data owner and custodian roles are defined formally in Module 12 with privacy obligations.
  • Why the module asks “when,” not “how.” Module 2 asked how tokenization works; this module asks when you would choose it over masking. That shift is what defines Domain 3.
  • Practice activities, labs, and the assessment. The classify-and-protect practice, method selection cases, recovery timeline, first-restore exercise, requirement-to-control map, labs, and the module quiz are only on the module page. The videos are a review aid, not a substitute.
