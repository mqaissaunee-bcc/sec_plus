Architecture Models and Enterprise Infrastructure

Deployment and compute models, specialised operational systems, network zones and device placement, and the secure communication methods that connect them.

Progress: 0% complete

Overview

[OBJECTIVES] By the end of this module you will be able to compare on-premises, cloud, and hybrid deployment against the considerations that drive the choice; distinguish virtualization, containers, serverless, and microservices; explain why specialised systems such as ICS, IoT, and embedded devices are difficult to secure; design network security zones and place devices correctly within them; and select secure communication methods including VPN, tunnelling, SD-WAN, and SASE.

Domain 3 is Security Architecture at 18% of the exam, and it changes the question being asked. Domain 2 asked what goes wrong. Domain 3 asks how you build things so that less goes wrong in the first place, and its questions are about choosing rather than identifying.

That distinction matters for how you study it. A Domain 2 question has a correct answer determined by fact - a worm is a worm. A Domain 3 question has a correct answer determined by constraints stated in the scenario. The same architecture is right in one situation and wrong in another, and the deciding detail is almost always something the question told you.

The considerations that drive every choice

Objective 3.1 names a list of considerations, and they function as the criteria by which architectures are compared. Learn them as questions to ask rather than terms to define.

Two of these carry more weight than the rest on the exam. Inability to patch is the defining constraint of the specialised systems later in this module, and it drives the compensating control answers you have seen since Module 1. Risk transference is the one students most often overstate: moving a workload to a provider transfers some responsibility, never accountability, and never data and access control.

🏗 Think of it like…

Choosing where to put a workshop

You can build your own workshop, rent a unit on an industrial estate, or hire a workshop by the hour when you need one. None is correct in the abstract. The right answer depends on how often you work, how much specialised equipment you need, whether the work is confidential, and whether you can afford the building.

Architecture questions work the same way. When a scenario mentions regulatory constraints, unpredictable demand, a tiny team, or a system that cannot be taken offline, it has just told you which criterion dominates - and that criterion picks the answer.

Deployment Models

On-premises

On-premises means the organization owns and operates the infrastructure in its own facilities. It provides maximum control: physical custody of the hardware, complete authority over configuration, and no dependence on a third party's decisions.

The costs are capital expenditure up front, capacity that must be provisioned for peak demand and sits idle otherwise, and full responsibility for every layer including physical security, power, and cooling. Scaling means buying and installing hardware, which is measured in weeks rather than minutes.

On-premises remains the right answer where a scenario mentions regulatory requirements mandating physical custody, data that cannot leave a jurisdiction, or systems needing deterministic performance the organization must guarantee itself.

Cloud

Cloud shifts infrastructure to a provider consumed as a service. Capacity scales on demand, cost becomes operating expense, and deployment is measured in minutes. The shared responsibility model from Module 4 applies: the provider secures layers according to the service model, and the customer always retains data and access control.

The trade-offs are dependence on the provider's availability and decisions, reduced visibility into layers you no longer operate, and costs that scale with use in ways that can surprise. Cloud is favoured where a scenario mentions unpredictable or spiky demand, rapid deployment, limited staff, or a preference for operating over capital expense.

Hybrid and third-party considerations

Hybrid combines both, typically keeping sensitive or regulated workloads on-premises while using cloud for scale, development, or overflow capacity. It is the most common real arrangement and the most complex to secure, because it introduces a boundary between two environments with different controls, different identity systems, and different logging.

The characteristic hybrid weakness is that boundary. Identity federated between environments, network links joining them, and data moving across all create exposure that neither environment has on its own. Exam scenarios describing a hybrid problem are usually describing something at the seam.

Third-party vendor considerations extend this. Every provider is a dependency you do not control, which connects to the supply chain material in Module 3 and the vendor assessment work that arrives in Module 12.

🚗 Think of it like…

Owning, leasing, and hailing a ride

Owning a car means full control, a large payment up front, and every repair is yours. Leasing gives you exclusive use with maintenance handled and a monthly cost. Hailing a ride means you pay only for journeys you take, someone else owns everything, and you have no say in the vehicle.

Notice what never transfers in any of them: what you carry in the car, and who you let in. However much of the vehicle someone else operates, your possessions and your passengers stay your responsibility - which is the shared responsibility model in one sentence.

Where it breaks down: you can inspect a hired car before getting in. You cannot inspect a provider's hypervisor, which is why cloud assurance relies on contracts, attestations, and audits rather than examination.

Centralized and decentralized

This is a separate axis from where infrastructure lives, and the exam treats it separately.

CentralizedDecentralized
ControlConsistent policy, one authorityLocal autonomy, varying practice
VisibilityOne place to lookFragmented
FailureA single point of failureFailures stay local
ResponsivenessSlower to local needsFaster locally
LatencyTraffic may travel farProcessing near the user

The security tension is real in both directions. Centralization gives consistent enforcement and complete logging, and concentrates risk - a compromise of the central authority affects everything. Decentralization contains failures and produces inconsistency, which is how gaps appear.

🏛 Think of it like…

Head office versus regional branches

With every decision made at head office, policy is uniform and anyone can audit the whole organization from one place. It is also slow for a branch needing something unusual, and a strike at head office stops everything.

With branches deciding for themselves, each responds quickly to local conditions and one branch's problems stay there. It is also nearly certain that two branches now do the same thing differently, and one of them does it badly - which nobody notices, because nobody is looking at all of them.

Virtualization, Containers, and Serverless

Virtualization

Virtualization runs multiple complete guest operating systems on one physical host, with a hypervisor allocating resources and enforcing isolation. Each guest has its own kernel and behaves as an independent machine.

The security properties follow from that structure. Isolation is strong because guests share only the hypervisor, and the hypervisor is a small, heavily scrutinised layer. The corresponding risk is VM escape from Module 4, and the operational risk is VM sprawl - machines are so easy to create that organizations accumulate ones nobody owns, which is the asset inventory problem again.

Containerization

Containers package an application with its dependencies but share the host operating system kernel rather than running a full guest OS. They start in milliseconds, use a fraction of the resources, and package consistently across environments.

The trade-off is isolation strength. Containers on a host share a kernel, so a kernel vulnerability can allow escape more readily than a hypervisor flaw allows VM escape. The exam expects you to know containers are lighter and faster, and that virtual machines provide stronger isolation - so workloads of genuinely different sensitivity belong on separate virtual machines rather than in separate containers on one host.

🍽 Think of it like…

Restaurants versus food trucks at a shared depot

A virtual machine is a restaurant with its own building, kitchen, plumbing, and staff. Expensive and slow to open, and what happens in one kitchen genuinely stays there.

A container is a food truck. It carries its own recipes, equipment, and ingredients so it works identically wherever it parks - but all the trucks plug into the same depot's power and water. They are quick to launch and cheap to run, and a contaminated water supply at the depot reaches every truck.

That shared supply is the shared kernel. It is what makes containers efficient and what makes their isolation weaker.

Serverless

Serverless runs individual functions in response to events, with the provider managing all underlying infrastructure. There are still servers; the customer simply has none to provision, patch, or scale.

This transfers substantial responsibility - no operating system to maintain, no capacity planning, and cost proportional to actual execution. What remains the customer's concentrates in three places: the function code, its dependencies, and above all the permissions granted to it. Over-permissioned functions are a leading cloud finding, because a broad policy applied during development is rarely narrowed afterwards.

Microservices

Microservices decompose an application into small independent services communicating over a network, in contrast to a monolith where all functionality lives in one deployable unit.

Benefits are independent deployment and scaling, fault isolation, and the ability for teams to work without coordinating releases. The security consequence is that communication which used to happen inside one process now crosses a network, which multiplies the attack surface and makes service-to-service authentication essential. A monolith has one front door; a microservice architecture has dozens of internal ones.

👨 Think of it like…

One chef versus a kitchen brigade

A single chef preparing an entire menu holds everything in their head and needs no coordination - but if they are ill, the restaurant closes, and changing one dish means one person changing everything.

A brigade of specialists can change the sauce without touching the pastry, and one absence does not close the kitchen. Now, though, every instruction between stations is spoken aloud in a room where anyone might be listening, and each station must recognise whether a request genuinely came from the head chef.

Infrastructure as code

Infrastructure as code defines infrastructure in machine-readable configuration files rather than through manual setup. Environments are created by executing a definition, so they are reproducible and can be rebuilt identically.

The security value is substantial. Configuration drift largely disappears because environments are rebuilt from a definition rather than modified in place. The definition goes into version control, so every change is attributable and reversible - which is the change management material from Module 1 applied to infrastructure. And security requirements can be encoded once and applied everywhere automatically.

The corresponding risk is that a flaw in a definition is reproduced perfectly across every environment it builds, and secrets committed into those files are exposed exactly as they were in the Module 2 key management failure. Definitions need review and scanning like any other code.

📜 Think of it like…

A written recipe versus cooking from memory

A cook working from memory produces a slightly different dish each time, and nobody can say exactly how today's differed from last Tuesday's. A written recipe produces the same dish every time, can be handed to someone else, and can be compared against an earlier version to see precisely what changed.

It also reproduces mistakes perfectly. A recipe with too much salt yields a hundred over-salted dishes rather than one - which is why infrastructure definitions get reviewed and scanned rather than merely trusted.

Specialised and Operational Systems

These systems share one property that shapes everything about securing them: the standard controls largely do not apply, and the reason is not neglect but genuine constraint.

ICS and SCADA

Industrial control systems operate physical processes - power generation, water treatment, manufacturing lines, building management. SCADA is the supervisory layer that monitors and controls them, often across geographically dispersed sites.

What makes them difficult is a genuine inversion of priorities. In conventional IT the ordering is roughly confidentiality, integrity, availability. In operational technology it is availability and safety first, because the system is controlling something physical. A turbine that stops because a patch required a reboot is not an inconvenience; it may be a safety incident.

The practical consequences follow directly. These systems run for decades, so a twenty-year-old controller is normal rather than negligent. They frequently cannot be patched, because the vendor no longer exists or because certification would have to be repeated. And they often use protocols designed before network security was a consideration, with no authentication or encryption at all.

⚕ Think of it like…

Maintenance during open-heart surgery

You would not upgrade the software on a heart-lung machine while it is running. Not because the update is unimportant, but because stopping the machine to apply it is categorically worse than the risk the update addresses.

Operational technology lives permanently in that state. "Just patch it" is not advice a plant engineer can act on, which is why the answers are segmentation, strict access control, and monitoring - controls that do not require touching the system itself.

IoT, embedded systems, and RTOS

TypeWhat it isCharacteristic weakness
IoTNetwork-connected everyday devicesDefault credentials, no update path, long service life
EmbeddedA computer inside a larger productNot perceived as a computer, so never inventoried
RTOSAn OS guaranteeing response within a time boundTiming constraints leave little room for security processing

A real-time operating system is defined by determinism rather than speed. It guarantees that a response occurs within a bounded time, which matters when the software is controlling an airbag or an anti-lock brake. That guarantee is what constrains security: operations with variable timing, including some cryptographic work, may be unacceptable.

IoT and embedded devices share a different problem. They are cheap, numerous, sold at margins that do not fund a decade of security updates, and frequently not recognised as computers at all. A building's HVAC controller, a networked printer, and a smart television are all general purpose computers on the network that nobody has listed as assets.

📺 Think of it like…

Appliances that quietly came with a phone line

Imagine discovering that your fridge, your thermostat, and your television were each installed with their own telephone line to the outside world. Nobody hid it - it was in the manual - but nobody thought of these things as having a connection, so nobody ever listed them, and none has been checked since the day it was fitted.

This is why IoT security starts with discovery rather than hardening. You cannot secure a device you have never counted, and these are the devices least likely to have been counted.

How specialised systems are actually secured

Because the systems themselves usually cannot be changed, the controls go around them.

ControlWhy it fits
Network segmentationSeparates operational technology from corporate IT entirely
Air gap or unidirectional gatewayRemoves the network path where the risk justifies it
Jump serverForces all administrative access through one controlled, logged point
Passive monitoringObserves without transmitting anything that could disturb the process
Strict access controlVery few people, very specific permissions
Compensating controlsThe standing answer when patching is impossible

Passive monitoring deserves emphasis because it is specific to this environment. Active scanning that would be routine on corporate IT can crash a fragile controller, so operational networks are typically monitored by observing a copy of the traffic rather than by probing devices.

Network Design and Device Placement

Security zones

A security zone is a portion of the network with a defined trust level and controlled boundaries. Traffic between zones passes through enforcement points; traffic within a zone generally does not.

ZoneContainsTrust
Untrusted / internetEverything outsideNone
Screened subnet (DMZ)Public-facing servicesLow
InternalCorporate workstations and serversModerate
RestrictedSensitive data, management interfacesHigh
Operational technologyICS, SCADA, building systemsIsolated

The screened subnet - historically called a DMZ - exists to hold services that must be reachable from the internet. Placing a web server there means an attacker who compromises it lands somewhere with no direct route into the internal network, so the compromise is contained rather than fatal.

✈ Think of it like…

Airport zones

Anyone may enter the terminal. Passing into departures requires a boarding pass and screening. Reaching the apron requires a staff pass and a specific reason. Each boundary has a checkpoint, and passing one does not grant the next.

The check-in desk is the screened subnet: deliberately accessible to the public, deliberately separated from the aircraft. Someone causing trouble there is a serious problem in the terminal and not a problem on the runway, which is exactly what zoning buys you.

Physical isolation and logical segmentation

Air-gapped systems have no network connection to anything else - the strongest isolation available, and appropriate for the most sensitive operational and classified environments. The cost is operational: updates and data must move by physical media, which is both laborious and itself a vector, as the removable media material in Module 3 showed.

Logical segmentation uses VLANs, subnets, and firewall policy to separate traffic on shared physical infrastructure. It is far more flexible and slightly weaker, since a misconfiguration can join what should be separate.

Software-defined networking separates the control plane that decides how traffic flows from the data plane that forwards it - the same architectural split as zero trust in Module 1. Policy becomes centrally programmable, which makes consistent segmentation practical at scale and makes the controller a high-value target.

Network appliances and where they go

ApplianceFunctionTypical placement
Jump serverSingle controlled entry point for administrationBetween user zone and restricted zone
Proxy serverMakes requests on a client's behalf; inspects and filtersBetween internal users and the internet
IDSDetects and alerts on suspicious trafficOut of band, on a tap or mirror port
IPSDetects and blocks suspicious trafficInline, in the traffic path
Load balancerDistributes traffic across serversIn front of a server pool
SensorsCollect traffic or telemetry for analysisAt zone boundaries and key segments

The IDS and IPS distinction is heavily tested and reduces to one thing: an IPS sits in the traffic path and can stop something; an IDS watches a copy and can only tell you. That placement difference determines everything else about them, including that an IPS failure affects traffic flow while an IDS failure does not.

👁 Think of it like…

A camera versus a guard in the doorway

A camera watching a corridor records everything and stops nothing. You learn what happened, possibly in time to react, and the corridor works normally whether or not the camera is functioning.

A guard standing in the doorway can refuse entry - and if the guard collapses, nobody gets through at all, including people who should. That is the IDS/IPS trade-off precisely, and it is why the next topic is what happens when the guard collapses.

Failure modes: fail-open and fail-closed

Every inline device must behave somehow when it fails, and the choice is a security decision rather than a technical one.

ModeOn failurePrioritises
Fail-openTraffic passes uninspectedAvailability
Fail-closedTraffic stopsSecurity

Neither is correct in general. A firewall protecting sensitive data should usually fail closed, because passing traffic uninspected is worse than passing none. A device in front of a safety-critical or life-supporting system may need to fail open, because an outage causes greater harm than uninspected traffic.

Exam scenarios supply the deciding detail. When the protected asset is sensitive data, fail closed. When it is availability of something people depend on physically, fail open is defensible.

🚪 Think of it like…

A fire door and a vault door

When the power fails, a fire door unlocks. Trapping people inside a burning building is worse than letting someone walk in - it fails open, for life safety.

When the power fails, a bank vault stays shut. Nobody is trapped inside, and an open vault is worse than an inaccessible one - it fails closed, for security.

Both are correct. They protect different things, which is exactly why the exam gives you the asset before asking for the mode.

Choosing a failure mode

Active, passive, inline, and tap

Two device attributes appear in the objective and are easy to conflate. Active versus passive describes whether a device transmits: an active scanner probes systems, while passive monitoring only listens. Inline versus tap describes placement: inline sits in the path, while a tap or mirror port receives a copy.

Passive and tap-based approaches are the standard answer for operational technology and other fragile environments, because they cannot disturb what they observe. They are also the reason such monitoring cannot block anything.

Port security: 802.1X and EAP

Physical network ports in accessible areas are an entry point, and 802.1X addresses this by requiring authentication before a port carries traffic. A device connecting is placed in a restricted state until it authenticates, typically against a RADIUS server. EAP is the framework carrying the authentication exchange, with variants supporting passwords, certificates, or tokens.

The security value is that plugging a cable in is no longer sufficient to be on the network. This is network access control applied at the port, and it addresses the unsecure wired network vector from Module 3.

Secure Communication and Control Selection

VPN and tunnelling

A VPN creates an encrypted tunnel across an untrusted network so traffic inside it is protected from interception and modification. Two arrangements matter for the exam.

TypeConnectsTypical use
Remote access VPNOne user to a networkStaff working remotely
Site-to-site VPNTwo networksBranch office to headquarters

A further distinction is full tunnel versus split tunnel. Full tunnel sends all traffic through the corporate network, so everything is inspected and policy applies uniformly, at the cost of bandwidth and latency. Split tunnel sends only corporate traffic through the tunnel and the rest directly to the internet, which performs better and means the device is simultaneously on the corporate network and an uninspected one.

The two tunnelling protocols named are IPSec, operating at the network layer and protecting all traffic between endpoints, and TLS, operating higher up and commonly used for browser-based remote access without a dedicated client.

🚚 Think of it like…

An armoured van on a public road

The road is public and anyone may drive on it or watch what passes. The van still travels that road - but its contents are sealed, and observers see only that a van went by.

Split tunnelling is the same van making a personal errand on the way, driving briefly without the armour. Nothing corporate is exposed in that moment, but the van is now in two worlds at once, and whatever it picks up on the errand comes back inside the depot.

SD-WAN and SASE

SD-WAN applies software-defined networking to connections between sites, choosing paths dynamically across whatever links are available. It reduces cost and improves resilience by treating several ordinary connections as one managed fabric.

SASE - secure access service edge - combines SD-WAN with security functions delivered from the cloud, so inspection and policy happen at a nearby point of presence rather than at headquarters.

The problem SASE solves is worth understanding, because it explains why the term exists. Traditional architecture backhauls all traffic to headquarters for inspection. When users are remote and applications are in the cloud, that means traffic travels to headquarters and back out again to reach a destination that was never on the corporate network - adding latency to protect a path nobody needed. SASE moves inspection to the edge, which is the architectural expression of zero trust from Module 1.

🗺 Think of it like…

Posting a local letter via head office

A branch in Lisbon wants to send a document to a client in Lisbon. Company policy requires all post to be checked at head office in Frankfurt, so the document flies to Frankfurt, is opened and inspected, and flies back to a recipient two streets away.

SASE opens an inspection office in Lisbon. The same check happens, with the same policy and the same records - the document simply stops crossing a continent twice to receive it.

Firewall types

TypeOperates onBest at
Layer 4 / packet filteringAddresses and portsFast, coarse rules
Layer 7 / applicationApplication contentUnderstanding what traffic actually does
NGFWApplication awareness, identity, threat intelligenceGeneral enterprise enforcement
WAFHTTP and HTTPS specificallyProtecting web applications from injection and XSS
UTMSeveral security functions in one applianceSmaller organizations wanting consolidation

The WAF is the one most often mismatched in exam answers. It protects web applications specifically, sitting in front of them and inspecting HTTP. It is the correct answer for web application attacks and the wrong answer for general network segmentation, where an NGFW belongs.

Recall from Module 4 that a WAF is a filter, so it inherits the weakness of all filters. It is valuable as defence in depth and as virtual patching, and it is not a substitute for parameterised queries.

Selecting effective controls

Objective 3.2 ends by asking you to select controls, which is the skill the whole module builds toward. A workable order:

StepQuestion
1What is being protected, and how sensitive is it?
2Which considerations does the scenario emphasise?
3What constraint has the scenario stated - cannot patch, cannot restart, cannot leave the jurisdiction?
4Does the control need to block, or only to observe?
5What happens when this control fails?

Step three does most of the work, as it did in Module 1. A stated constraint eliminates whole categories of answer, and the remaining option is usually the intended one.

[EXAM] Three quick discriminators. IDS observes and IPS blocks, which follows entirely from inline versus tap placement. Fail-closed protects data and fail-open protects availability, so read what the asset is. And a WAF protects web applications specifically - if the scenario is about network segmentation, it is the wrong appliance.
