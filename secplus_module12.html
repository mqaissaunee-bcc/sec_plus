Third-Party Risk, Compliance, Audits, and Awareness

Assessing and monitoring vendors, compliance reporting and its consequences, privacy and data subject rights, audits and penetration testing, and building an awareness programme that changes behaviour.

Progress: 0% complete

Overview

[OBJECTIVES] By the end of this module you will be able to assess and monitor third-party risk and identify the right agreement type; explain compliance reporting, monitoring, and the consequences of failure; apply privacy concepts including data subject rights and retention; distinguish internal from external audits and the penetration testing variants; and design a security awareness programme that changes behaviour.

This module completes Domain 5 and the course. It covers four objectives that share a single premise: security depends on people and organizations outside your direct control - your suppliers, your regulators, your auditors, and every member of staff.

Everything in the previous eleven modules is a control you operate. Everything here concerns assurance about things you do not operate, which is a different problem requiring different instruments: contracts, audits, questionnaires, and training rather than firewalls and patches.

The idea that unifies the four

Accountability does not transfer. Module 11 established this for insurance and outsourcing, and it is the thread through everything here. A supplier's breach is your incident. A regulator's finding lands on your organization, not your auditor's. And an employee who clicks a link creates an exposure the organization owns.

What these objectives describe is how an organization gains reasonable confidence about things it cannot directly control - and what it does when that confidence turns out to be misplaced.

🗝 Think of it like…

The cleaner's copy of your key

You can fit the best locks available, set the alarm every time, and never leave a window open. You have also given a key to a cleaning company whose hiring practices you have never asked about, whose key cabinet you have never seen, and whose staff turnover you do not know.

Your house is now exactly as secure as their weakest process. Nothing about your own locks has changed, and nothing about them helps - which is the entire subject of third-party risk in one sentence.

Third-Party Risk

Assessing a vendor

Objective 5.3 names several assessment instruments, and they differ sharply in how much assurance they actually provide.

InstrumentWhat it gives youAssurance
QuestionnaireThe vendor's own answers about its practicesLowest - self-declared
Evidence of internal auditsThe vendor's own review findingsLow - the vendor chose what to share
Right-to-audit clauseContractual entitlement to inspectModerate - if actually exercised
Independent assessmentThird-party review against a standardHigh
Penetration testingDemonstrated resistance to attackHigh, and point-in-time
Supply chain analysisVisibility of the vendor's own dependenciesAddresses the fourth-party problem

The ordering matters more than the list. Questionnaires are the most common instrument and the weakest, because they ask an organization to grade itself with a contract at stake. They are still worth sending - the answers create a documented record and reveal which vendors cannot answer basic questions - but treating a completed questionnaire as assurance is the mistake the exam probes.

A right-to-audit clause is only as useful as its exercise. Organizations negotiate the clause, congratulate themselves, and never invoke it - which delivers exactly the assurance of not having it, with more paperwork.

Supply chain analysis addresses what is sometimes called fourth-party risk: your vendor's vendors. Module 3 established that attackers target the weakest link, and that link is frequently two steps away from you and entirely invisible without deliberately asking.

🍳 Think of it like…

Self-declared hygiene versus an inspection

A restaurant filling in its own form about kitchen cleanliness will report a clean kitchen. Not necessarily dishonestly - people genuinely believe their own practices are fine, and nobody scrutinises what they do every day.

An environmental health officer arriving unannounced produces different information, and the difference is not the questions. It is who is answering them and whether anyone checked.

Questionnaire against evidence

Vendor selection

Due diligence is the investigation performed before entering a relationship: financial stability, security posture, regulatory history, and whether the vendor can actually do what it claims. It is distinct from due care, which is the ongoing exercise of reasonable prudence once the relationship exists. Due diligence is investigation; due care is maintenance.

Conflict of interest appears in the objective because selection decisions are vulnerable to influences that have nothing to do with capability - an existing relationship, a personal connection, or a selector who stands to benefit. The control is disclosure and separation: anyone with an interest declares it and steps out of the decision.

🤝 Think of it like…

Checking before you hire, and after

Before taking on a builder you check their references, look at previous work, and confirm they are insured. That is due diligence, and it happens once, before anything is signed.

Once they start, you look at the work as it goes up, ask about the bits that seem wrong, and notice when the insurance certificate expires. That is due care, and it never stops.

Organizations are usually good at the first and forget the second, because diligence produces a document and care produces nothing visible until something has already gone wrong.

Agreement types

These are heavily tested and easily confused. The distinguishing question is what each document is for.

TypeFull namePurpose
SLAService level agreementDefines measurable service standards and what happens if they are missed
MSAMaster service agreementOverarching terms governing an ongoing relationship
SOW / WOStatement of work / work orderThe specific work, deliverables, and timeline under an MSA
MOUMemorandum of understandingStates intent to cooperate; generally not binding
MOAMemorandum of agreementMore formal than an MOU; may carry obligations
NDANon-disclosure agreementProtects confidential information shared between parties
BPABusiness partnership agreementGoverns the terms of a partnership between organizations

Two relationships resolve most questions. MSA and SOW work together: the MSA is negotiated once and sets the terms, and each piece of work gets an SOW describing what will be delivered. That is why a scenario about defining deliverables for a specific project points to an SOW rather than to renegotiating the MSA.

And an MOU is generally not binding, which is the distinction most often tested. If a scenario requires enforceable commitments with consequences, an MOU is the wrong answer.

Practice: choose the agreement

Monitoring and rules of engagement

Vendor monitoring is ongoing rather than periodic reassessment at renewal. A vendor assessed thoroughly in 2023 may since have been acquired, moved its hosting, suffered a breach, or lost the staff who ran its security programme. Continuous or at least recurring review is the same argument Module 11 made for continuous risk assessment.

Rules of engagement define the boundaries of an assessment or test before it begins: what is in scope, what is explicitly out, when testing may occur, what techniques are permitted, and who to contact if something breaks. They protect both parties, and their absence is how a penetration test becomes an incident, a legal dispute, or an outage nobody expected.

Compliance

Reporting and monitoring

Compliance reporting is internal - to management, audit committees, and boards - or external, to regulators, customers, and certification bodies. The audiences differ in what they can do with the information, which is the Module 9 reporting lesson again.

Compliance monitoring is the ongoing verification that requirements are actually being met, and the objective names its components.

ComponentWhat it means
Due diligenceInvestigating before committing
Due careExercising ongoing reasonable prudence
AttestationA formal statement that a requirement is met
AcknowledgementConfirmation that staff have received and understood policy
Internal and externalSelf-verification and independent verification
AutomationContinuous technical checking rather than periodic manual review

Acknowledgement is the element that connects to Module 11's acceptable use policy. A signed acknowledgement is what demonstrates the organization told someone what was expected, which is what makes enforcement defensible.

Due diligence and due care, precisely

These two are examined as a pair and are easy to blur, because in ordinary speech both mean "being careful". In compliance they mark different moments.

Due diligence is investigation before commitment - checking a vendor's financial stability and security posture before signing, or assessing a regulatory requirement before entering a market. Due care is the ongoing exercise of reasonable prudence afterwards: monitoring the vendor, maintaining the controls, reviewing the policy. Diligence answers "should we?"; care answers "are we still?".

The legal weight of the distinction is why it appears in the objective. An organization that investigated properly and then did nothing for four years has demonstrated diligence and failed at care - and it is failure of the second that regulators encounter most often, because the first produces a document and the second produces nothing visible until something goes wrong.

Automating compliance monitoring

Manual compliance checking has the same defect as the annual risk assessment from Module 11: it describes a moment that has already passed. An organization audited as compliant in March may have drifted by May, and nothing detects it until the next review.

Automated monitoring applies the Module 8 configuration-enforcement approach to compliance requirements. Technical controls that map to a requirement - encryption enabled, logging retained for the mandated period, access reviews completed on schedule - can be checked continuously and reported as a live position rather than a periodic snapshot.

The limit is worth stating: not every requirement is technically measurable. Whether staff understood a policy, whether a supplier's practices match its claims, and whether a risk decision was appropriate all require human judgement. Automation handles the checkable majority and frees attention for the part that cannot be automated - which is the same argument Module 10 made for automation generally.

Consequences of non-compliance

Consequences of non-compliance

ConsequenceNature
FinesDirect financial penalty, often scaled to revenue
SanctionsRestrictions on how the organization may operate
Reputational damageLoss of customer and partner confidence
Loss of licenceRemoval of permission to operate at all
Contractual impactsBreach of customer agreements, lost contracts, liability

Loss of licence is the consequence that reframes the others. Fines are survivable and reputational damage recovers over time. An organization that cannot lawfully trade has no path back, which is why Module 11's example of an expansionary appetite with near-zero tolerance for regulatory risk is coherent rather than contradictory.

⭐ Think of it like…

A hygiene rating in the window

A poor rating costs a restaurant custom immediately, and everyone walking past can see it. That is reputational damage, and it recovers slowly if the kitchen improves.

Closure by the council is a different category. There is no gradual recovery from being forbidden to open, and no amount of subsequent good practice trades against it. That is what loss of licence means, and it is why some risks are treated as absolute regardless of appetite elsewhere.

Privacy

The data subject and the roles

A data subject is the living individual the personal data is about. This is the role Module 11 did not cover, and it matters because privacy law grants rights to the subject rather than to the organization holding the data.

The controller and processor distinction from Module 11 applies directly here: the controller decides the purpose and carries the primary obligations to the subject, and the processor acts on instructions. When a data subject exercises a right, they exercise it against the controller, who must then ensure its processors comply.

The objective also names ownership, and this is where an intuition needs correcting. An organization holding personal data does not own it in the sense of being free to do as it wishes. It holds data about a person, under obligations to that person, for purposes it declared.

🧾 Think of it like…

Who the receipt belongs to

A shop keeps a record of what you bought. The record is in their system, written by their staff, on their premises - and it is about you. Both facts are true at once, and the second is the one privacy law builds on.

That is why 'ownership' is the wrong frame for personal data. An organization holds data about a person, under obligations to that person, for purposes it declared. Possession is not the same as freedom to do as it likes with it.

Legal implications across jurisdictions

Privacy requirements arrive at local, regional, national, and global levels simultaneously, and they do not agree. An organization operating internationally faces different definitions of personal data, different lawful bases for processing, different breach notification deadlines, and different rules on transferring data across borders.

The practical consequence connects to Module 7's sovereignty material: where data physically sits determines which laws apply, and cloud replication can move it without anyone deciding to. Privacy compliance is therefore an architectural constraint rather than a documentation exercise.

📦 Think of it like…

The boxes in the loft

Everyone knows roughly what is in the loft. Then the roof leaks and the insurer asks for a list, and it turns out nobody can say what is in the boxes at the back, when they went up, or whether anything up there still needs keeping.

A data inventory is that list, written before the leak. Every privacy obligation - answering a subject's request, deleting on schedule, reporting a breach accurately - depends on it, and an organization without one cannot even establish what it has lost.

Data inventory and retention

Every privacy obligation depends on knowing what personal data you hold, where it is, why you have it, and how long you intend to keep it. This is the asset inventory problem from Module 8 applied to data, and it fails the same way - through copies in places nobody catalogued.

Retention was covered in Module 7, and the privacy dimension sharpens it: personal data should be kept no longer than necessary for the purpose it was collected for. Data retained beyond that is a liability with no offsetting value, and it is the material most likely to appear in a breach that nobody needed to be exposed to.

The right to be forgotten

The right to be forgotten - more precisely a right to erasure - allows a data subject to request deletion of their personal data in defined circumstances. It is not absolute: it yields where there is a legal obligation to retain, an ongoing contract, or a legal claim.

Its technical difficulty is the part worth understanding. Deleting a record from a production database is straightforward. Locating the same person's data in backups, log files, analytics platforms, data warehouses, and a processor's systems is not - and an organization without the data inventory above cannot answer the request completely, or even know whether it has.

This is also where Module 10's legal hold interacts: a live hold can override an erasure request, and reconciling the two is a legal determination rather than a technical one.

📚 Think of it like…

Asking a library to remove your name

A library agrees to delete your membership record. Straightforward - one entry in one system.

Then you remember the borrowing history in the archive, the newsletter list held by the mailing company, the door-entry logs, the photograph in last year's annual report, and the spreadsheet a volunteer keeps for the reading group. None of those were hidden. Nobody ever wrote down that they existed.

Where it breaks down: a library could reasonably search everywhere in a day. An organization holding personal data across hundreds of systems and several processors cannot, which is why the data inventory is the precondition for the right rather than an administrative nicety.

Audits, Assessments, and Penetration Testing

Internal and external

TypePerformed byPrimary value
Self-assessmentThe team that operates the controlCheap, frequent, lowest independence
Internal auditAn internal function independent of operationsIndependent within the organization
Audit committeeA board-level body overseeing auditEnsures findings reach people with authority
External / third-party auditAn independent firmCredible to outside parties
Regulatory examinationA regulatorCompulsory; carries enforcement power

The distinguishing property is independence, and it rises down that list. A self-assessment is performed by people evaluating their own work, which is useful for finding problems and worthless as assurance to anyone else. An external audit carries weight precisely because the auditor has no stake in the answer.

The audit committee exists to solve a structural problem: findings that embarrass management tend not to reach the board if they travel through management. Reporting to a board-level committee gives internal audit a route that does not depend on the goodwill of the people being audited.

Attestation in this context is a formal statement, often by an independent party, that something is as claimed. It is what a customer receives instead of being allowed to audit a provider directly.

📋 Think of it like…

Counting your own stock

A shopkeeper counting the stockroom finds genuine discrepancies and fixes them. Useful work, and no bank lending against that inventory would accept the shopkeeper's own count as evidence.

An external stocktaker produces a number of identical accuracy and entirely different standing, because the difference was never about arithmetic. It was about who had an interest in the result.

🔐 Think of it like…

Whether the locksmith gets the floorplan

Hire a locksmith to test your building and tell them nothing, and you learn what a stranger could manage in a week. Much of that week goes on working out where the doors are.

Give them the plans, the key schedule, and access to the alarm panel, and they spend the whole week on locks rather than on cartography. You will hear about far more weaknesses.

Neither is more rigorous. They answer different questions - and an organization that wants to know what is actually wrong, rather than what a stranger would find first, usually wants the plans handed over.

Penetration testing variants

Objective 5.5 names several dimensions, and they are independent of each other.

DimensionVariants
Knowledge givenKnown, partially known, unknown environment
PostureOffensive, defensive, integrated
TargetPhysical, network, application, personnel
ReconnaissancePassive, active

The knowledge spectrum replaces the older black, grey, and white box terminology. An unknown environment test simulates an external attacker with no inside information, and spends much of its budget on discovery. A known environment test provides full documentation and access, which finds more issues per pound because no time is spent rediscovering what the organization already knows. A partially known test sits between them.

Which is appropriate depends on the question being asked. To learn what an external attacker could achieve, restrict the knowledge. To find as many weaknesses as possible, provide everything - and organizations wanting genuine assurance usually want the second, however much the first appeals.

Integrated testing runs offensive and defensive teams together so that the defenders learn to detect what the attackers do, rather than receiving a report weeks later.

Reconnaissance

Passive reconnaissance gathers information without interacting with the target: public records, job advertisements revealing technologies in use, social media, DNS records, and published documents. It is undetectable by the target because nothing touches their systems.

Active reconnaissance interacts directly - scanning ports, probing services, enumerating hosts. It yields far more and is detectable, and it is the point at which authorisation genuinely matters, which is why rules of engagement are agreed first.

🔎 Think of it like…

Reading the brochure or trying the doors

Studying a building's public planning application, its published opening hours, and photographs of the lobby tells you a great deal, and nobody inside has any way of knowing you did it. That is passive reconnaissance.

Walking round the building trying each door tells you considerably more. It is also visible on camera, and doing it without written permission is a different activity with a different name - which is precisely why rules of engagement exist.

Scoping a test

Security Awareness

Objective 5.6 is the last on the exam and, in practice, one of the most consequential. Module 3 established that social engineering targets people because people are reachable regardless of technical controls. Awareness is the control applied to that surface.

Phishing campaigns

Simulated phishing sends staff realistic but harmless phishing messages to measure and improve recognition. Three points determine whether a campaign helps or harms.

The click rate is the less important number. The report rate matters more, because an organization where thirty percent click and sixty percent report is in a far stronger position than one where five percent click and nobody says anything. Reporting is what gives the security team the chance to act before the payload runs.

Punitive campaigns backfire. Naming and shaming people who click produces staff who conceal mistakes, which is precisely the behaviour that turns a click into an undetected breach. This is the blameless principle from Modules 3 and 10 applied to training.

Responding to reports is part of the programme. Staff who report suspicious messages and hear nothing back stop reporting. Acknowledgement, and telling people what the message turned out to be, sustains the behaviour the campaign exists to build.

🚨 Think of it like…

A fire drill nobody is punished for

A building runs a drill and someone takes four minutes to leave. The useful response is finding out why - a jammed door, an unclear sign, a route nobody had walked. The useless response is disciplining them, after which the next drill produces people who look prompt and a building nobody has learned anything about.

Simulated phishing works the same way. The click is information. What an organization does with it determines whether it gets more information next time or less.

🚶 Think of it like…

The colleague who takes a shortcut across the car park

Someone consistently cuts across the car park instead of using the marked path. You can put up another sign, or you can notice that the marked path adds four minutes and goes the wrong way round a locked gate.

Risky behaviour is usually rational from where the person is standing. The sign treats the walking; the gate is why they walk there. Reinforcing a policy without addressing what made the shortcut attractive produces the same behaviour, taken more discreetly.

Recognising anomalous behaviour

The objective distinguishes three kinds, and the distinction shapes the response.

TypeDescriptionResponse
RiskyKnowingly taking a shortcut - sharing a password, disabling a controlPolicy reinforcement; understand why the shortcut appealed
UnexpectedActivity outside a person's normal patternInvestigate; may be compromise or may be legitimate
UnintentionalGenuine mistake - wrong recipient, misconfigurationProcess and design improvement, not blame

Risky behaviour deserves the most thought, because it is usually rational. Someone shares a password because the approved process takes three days and their work is due tomorrow. Reinforcing the policy without fixing the three days produces the same behaviour, conducted more discreetly.

📢 Think of it like…

What the recruitment advert gives away

A job advert saying the team runs a specific version of a specific product, integrates with three named systems, and is migrating off a fourth is an honest, well-written advert. It is also a free technology inventory for anyone who reads it.

None of that is secret and none of it needs to be published together. Operational security is the habit of noticing what an ordinary, reasonable disclosure adds up to when someone collects all of them - which is exactly the passive reconnaissance described earlier in this module.

Development, execution, and measurement

The objective treats a programme as having a lifecycle. Development builds material suited to the actual risks and the actual audience. Execution delivers it, initially at onboarding and recurrently thereafter. Reporting and monitoring measures whether behaviour changed.

That last point is where most programmes fail. Completion rates measure attendance rather than effect. The measures that matter are behavioural: phishing report rates, time to report, incidents caused by avoidable error, and whether staff raise concerns at all. A programme with a hundred percent completion rate and a two percent report rate is not working, however good the compliance figure looks.

🎓 Think of it like…

Everyone attended the swimming lesson

A school reports that every pupil attended the swimming course. The figure is accurate and answers a question nobody asked. What anyone actually wants to know is how many can now swim.

Completion rates are attendance. Report rates, time to report, and avoidable-error incidents are the equivalent of getting in the water - and they are the only numbers that tell you whether the programme did anything.

An MOU is generally not binding, so it is wrong wherever enforceability is required. And in awareness programmes the report rate matters more than the click rate.
