Cryptographic Solutions

Symmetric and asymmetric encryption, hashing and salting, public key infrastructure, obfuscation, and the key management failures that cause most real cryptographic breaches.

Progress: 0% complete

Overview

[OBJECTIVES] By the end of this module you will be able to distinguish symmetric from asymmetric encryption and say which key does what; explain hashing, salting, and key stretching and why passwords are never encrypted; trace a certificate chain from a root of trust to a server certificate and explain how revocation works; compare obfuscation, tokenization, masking, and steganography; and identify where keys are generated, stored, and destroyed.

Cryptography is objective 1.4, a single objective inside the smallest domain on the exam. It is also the single densest body of vocabulary in Security+, and it produces more wrong answers than any other topic in Domain 1. The reason is that students memorise definitions without understanding which key performs which operation, and the exam is built almost entirely around that distinction.

Here is the shape of the problem. Nearly every candidate can recite that asymmetric cryptography uses a public key and a private key. Far fewer can answer, without hesitating, which key encrypts a message intended to stay confidential, and which key signs a message so the recipient knows who sent it. Those are different keys belonging to different people, and a question that hinges on it will offer both as options.

What cryptography actually provides

Map each tool onto the security properties from Module 1. This table is worth learning before anything else in the module, because it converts a memorisation problem into a reasoning problem.

ToolProvidesDoes not provide
Symmetric encryptionConfidentialityIntegrity, non-repudiation
Asymmetric encryptionConfidentialityIntegrity on its own

Read the third row carefully. Hashing gives you integrity but not non-repudiation, because anyone can compute a hash. That distinction was introduced in Module 1 and is tested repeatedly here. Read the fourth row too: a digital signature does not hide anything. A signed message is fully readable by anyone; the signature only proves who wrote it and that it has not changed.

[EXAM] When a scenario asks for both secrecy and proof of origin, the answer involves two operations, not one. You sign, then you encrypt. Options offering a single mechanism for both are distractors.

Symmetric and Asymmetric Encryption

Symmetric encryption

One key both encrypts and decrypts. It is fast, well understood, and does the overwhelming majority of real encryption work: your disk encryption, your VPN tunnel, and the bulk of every TLS session are all symmetric.

The dominant algorithm is AES, standardised in 128-, 192-, and 256-bit key lengths. You should recognise AES as the modern default and DES and 3DES as deprecated predecessors that appear in exam questions as the wrong answer. Symmetric ciphers operate either as block ciphers, processing fixed-size chunks, or stream ciphers, processing a bit or byte at a time. AES is a block cipher.

🏠 Think of it like…

A house key you have copied

You and your housemate hold identical keys. Either of you can lock the door and either can unlock it, which is efficient and simple. But the arrangement only works because you were both physically present when the copy was cut.

Now imagine you need to give a key to someone across the country who you have never met. You cannot post it, because anyone handling the envelope could copy it. That is the key distribution problem, and it is the reason asymmetric cryptography had to be invented.

The key distribution problem

Symmetric encryption assumes both parties already share a secret. For two people who have met, that is easy. For a browser connecting to a server it has never contacted, on a network operated by strangers, it is impossible - you cannot send the key over the channel you are trying to protect, because anyone listening would capture it.

Scale makes it worse. Ten people who all need to communicate privately in pairs require 45 separate keys. A thousand people require nearly half a million. Symmetric cryptography alone does not scale to the open internet.

Asymmetric encryption

Asymmetric cryptography uses a mathematically linked pair of keys. What one key does, only the other can undo. One key is published freely - the public key - and the other is never shared with anyone - the private key. Knowing the public key does not let you derive the private key.

The dominant algorithms are RSA and elliptic curve cryptography (ECC). ECC achieves comparable security with much shorter keys, which is why it dominates mobile and embedded use where processing power and bandwidth are constrained. Diffie-Hellman is a related mechanism used specifically to agree on a shared secret over an untrusted channel.

📫 Think of it like…

A mailbox with a public slot

Anyone walking past can drop a letter through the slot - the slot is public, and you can advertise its location on a billboard. But only you hold the key that opens the box and retrieves what is inside.

That is asymmetric encryption exactly. Publishing the slot's location does not help a thief, because knowing where to post a letter tells you nothing about how to open the box. This solves the distribution problem: you can safely broadcast your public key to strangers.

Where it breaks down: a real mailbox holds letters until you collect them. Asymmetric encryption is also slow - hundreds of times slower than symmetric - so it is almost never used to encrypt bulk data. It is used to exchange a symmetric key, and then symmetric encryption does the actual work.

The rule that answers most exam questions

Four operations, four keys. If you learn nothing else from this section, learn this table.

GoalOperationKey usedWhose key
Send a secretEncryptPublicThe recipient's
Read the secretDecryptPrivateThe recipient's
Prove authorshipSignPrivateThe sender's
Check authorshipVerifyPublicThe sender's

The pattern beneath it: encryption uses the recipient's keys, signing uses the sender's. And in both cases the public key does the thing anyone may do (send secretly, check a signature) while the private key does the thing only the owner may do (read the secret, create the signature).

[EXAM] The most common error is answering that a sender encrypts with their own public key. That would be pointless - anyone could then decrypt it with the sender's widely published public key. If a message is meant for Bob's eyes only, it is locked with Bob's public key, because only Bob's private key can open it.

Hybrid cryptography: how it works in practice

Real systems use both. Asymmetric cryptography solves the introduction; symmetric cryptography does the work. Every HTTPS connection you make follows this pattern:

1. Client and server use asymmetric crypto to agree on a shared secret 2. That shared secret becomes a symmetric session key 3. All actual page data is encrypted symmetrically with that session key 4. The session key is discarded when the connection closes

This is why questions describing "bulk data encryption" or "encrypting a large file" point to symmetric answers, while questions about "establishing a secure channel with a new party" point to asymmetric.

Perfect forward secrecy extends this: a fresh session key is negotiated for every connection and never reused, so an attacker who later steals the server's private key still cannot decrypt yesterday's recorded traffic.

Practice: pick the primitive

Read each requirement and choose the tool and the property it delivers.

Key length and algorithm strength

Longer keys mean more possible values and therefore more work to brute force. But key lengths are not comparable across algorithm families: a 256-bit ECC key is roughly equivalent to a 3072-bit RSA key, because they rest on different mathematical problems. A question comparing a 256-bit symmetric key to a 2048-bit RSA key and asking which is stronger is testing whether you know the comparison is invalid.

Hashing, Salting, and Key Stretching

What a hash is

A hash function takes input of any size and produces a fixed-length output. The same input always produces the same output, and any change to the input - even one bit - produces a completely different result. Critically, the function runs one way only: you cannot work backwards from a hash to recover the input.

Common algorithms are SHA-256 and SHA-512, both from the SHA-2 family, with SHA-3 available and MD5 and SHA-1 both deprecated. MD5 and SHA-1 are broken because practical collisions have been found - two different inputs producing the same hash - which destroys the integrity guarantee. Expect them as wrong answers.

🧬 Think of it like…

Blending a smoothie

Put in specific fruit and you get a specific smoothie. Put in exactly the same fruit tomorrow and you get an identical smoothie. Swap one strawberry for a raspberry and the result is visibly different.

But no amount of skill reconstructs the original fruit from the glass. That is what one-way means, and it is why hashing is not encryption - encryption is designed to be reversed by whoever holds the key, and hashing is designed to be irreversible by anyone.

Where it breaks down: two different fruit combinations could plausibly yield a similar-looking smoothie. In a sound hash function that is a collision, and it is considered a fatal flaw rather than an acceptable coincidence - which is exactly why MD5 was retired.

Why passwords are hashed and never encrypted

This is a high-yield distinction. Encryption is reversible by design, so a system that encrypts passwords must store the decryption key somewhere, and an attacker who reaches the database can usually reach the key too. Hashing has no key to steal.

When you set a password, the system stores only its hash. When you log in, the system hashes what you typed and compares. It never needs to know your actual password, which is why a well-built service genuinely cannot tell you what your password is - only reset it.

[RED FLAG] If a service emails you your existing password, it is storing it in a recoverable form. That is a finding in any security assessment, and on the exam it is always the wrong practice.

Salting and rainbow tables

Plain hashing has a weakness. Because the same input always gives the same output, two users with the password Summer2026 have identical hashes in the database. Attackers exploit this with rainbow tables: enormous precomputed lists of common passwords and their hashes. Cracking becomes a lookup rather than a computation.

A salt is a unique random value generated per user and combined with the password before hashing. Two users with identical passwords now have completely different stored hashes. The salt is not secret - it is stored alongside the hash - because its purpose is not concealment. Its purpose is to make precomputation useless, since an attacker would need a separate rainbow table for every possible salt.

🍳 Think of it like…

A secret ingredient in every portion

Imagine a caterer who adds a different rare spice to each individual meal. Two customers ordering the identical dish receive plates that taste measurably different. An industrial spy with a catalogue of standard recipes cannot match either plate to a known dish, because no catalogue anticipated that spice.

The spice is not hidden - it is printed on the ticket. It does not need to be secret, because its job is to make the attacker's precomputed catalogue worthless. They must now analyse every plate individually, which is precisely the economics a salt is designed to impose.

The avalanche effect and salting

Key stretching

Modern hardware computes billions of SHA-256 hashes per second, so even salted hashes of weak passwords fall to brute force. Key stretching answers this by making the hash deliberately slow and memory-hungry, applying the function thousands or millions of times.

The names to recognise are PBKDF2, bcrypt, scrypt, and Argon2. These are the correct answers for password storage. Plain SHA-256 is a correct answer for file integrity but a wrong answer for password storage, and the exam tests that difference directly.

The asymmetry is the point: a legitimate login can afford 200 milliseconds, but that same cost multiplied across billions of guesses makes large-scale cracking economically impractical.

🔒 Think of it like…

A deliberately stiff lock

A lock that takes a full second to turn is mildly annoying when you come home twice a day. For a burglar trying thousands of keys in sequence, that same second is ruinous - what would have taken an hour now takes weeks.

Key stretching engineers exactly this asymmetry. The cost is negligible for the one legitimate use and prohibitive for the attacker's millions.

HMAC: hashing with a key

A plain hash proves the data has not changed, but not who produced it - an attacker who alters a file can simply recompute its hash. HMAC (hash-based message authentication code) fixes this by mixing a shared secret key into the hash. Only someone holding the key can generate a valid HMAC.

Note the ladder carefully, because the exam tests each rung:

MechanismIntegrityAuthenticationNon-repudiation
HashYesNoNo
HMACYesYesNo
Digital signatureYesYesYes

HMAC stops at authentication because the key is shared: either party could have produced the code, so neither can prove the other did it. A digital signature uses a private key held by exactly one party, which is what adds non-repudiation.

Public Key Infrastructure

The problem PKI solves

Asymmetric cryptography lets you encrypt a message with someone's public key. It does not tell you whether the public key you just downloaded actually belongs to them. An attacker who substitutes their own public key for your bank's receives everything you thought you were sending securely - and you would notice nothing, because the encryption works perfectly. It is simply protecting the wrong conversation.

Public key infrastructure is the system of authorities, certificates, and procedures that binds a public key to a verified identity, so that you can trust a key belongs to who it claims.

🗳 Think of it like…

A passport, not a homemade ID card

Anyone can print a card with their photograph and name on it. A border officer will not accept it, because the card vouches for nothing beyond the claim of the person holding it. A passport is accepted because a government the officer already trusts verified the identity and issued the document with features that are hard to forge.

A certificate authority is that government. It checks that you control the domain you claim, then issues a certificate binding your public key to that name. Browsers accept it because they already trust the authority - not because they know anything about you.

Where it breaks down: you hold one passport from one country. A browser ships with a trust store of well over a hundred root authorities, and a compromise at any of them can produce a certificate your browser will accept for any site.

The chain of trust

Trust is not established directly. It is delegated down a chain from a small number of root authorities, through intermediates, to the certificate on an individual server.

Root CA private keys are extraordinarily valuable, so they are kept offline in physically secured hardware and used rarely. Roots sign intermediate certificates, and intermediates do the day-to-day work of signing server certificates. If an intermediate is compromised it can be revoked without invalidating the root and every certificate beneath it.

When your browser validates a certificate it walks this chain upward, checking at each step that the signature is valid, the certificate has not expired, the name matches the site, and the certificate has not been revoked. It stops when it reaches a root already in its trust store. If the chain cannot be completed to a trusted root, you get a warning.

Certificate lifecycle

StageWhat happens
Key generationThe subject generates a key pair; the private key never leaves their control
CSRA certificate signing request carries the public key and identity details to the CA
ValidationThe CA verifies the requester controls the domain or organization
IssuanceThe CA signs the certificate with its own private key
DeploymentThe certificate is installed on the server
RenewalCertificates expire deliberately, forcing periodic revalidation

Note what the CSR does not contain: the private key. A CSR carries only the public key and identifying information. An exam option describing a CSR that transmits the private key to the CA is always wrong.

Certificate validation walkthrough

Revocation: CRL and OCSP

A certificate is valid until it expires - unless it is revoked first, which happens when a private key is stolen or a certificate was issued in error. Two mechanisms communicate revocation.

A certificate revocation list (CRL) is a signed list of revoked serial numbers published periodically by the CA. It is simple but grows large and is only as current as the last publication.

OCSP (Online Certificate Status Protocol) queries the CA about one specific certificate in real time. It is current, but it makes the CA a dependency on every connection and leaks which sites a user visits. OCSP stapling resolves both problems: the server periodically fetches a signed, timestamped status for its own certificate and presents it during the handshake, so the client gets fresh status without contacting the CA.

💳 Think of it like…

A cancelled credit card

Your card carries an expiry date, but it can also be cancelled long before it. Merchants once consulted a printed booklet of cancelled card numbers delivered weekly - a certificate revocation list. A card cancelled on Tuesday still worked until the next booklet arrived.

Modern terminals phone the bank for each transaction, which is OCSP. And OCSP stapling is the merchant obtaining a dated letter from the bank each morning confirming the card is good, then showing that letter to the customer - current enough to be useful, without a call every time.

A wildcard certificate for *.example.com covers mail.example.com and vpn.example.com but not example.com itself, and not a.b.example.com. Its convenience carries a real cost: one stolen private key compromises every subdomain at once.

Self-signed certificates provide encryption but no verified identity, which is why browsers warn on them. They are appropriate for internal systems where an organization runs its own CA and distributes its root to managed devices - not for anything public-facing.

Obfuscation and Data Protection

Encryption is not the only way to reduce the value of data. This family of techniques hides, replaces, or conceals information, and the exam tests whether you can tell them apart - they look similar in a list and behave very differently.

Steganography

Steganography conceals the existence of a message rather than its contents. Data is hidden inside an ordinary-looking carrier - subtle adjustments to the pixels of an image, unused audio frequencies, or whitespace in a document - so that a casual observer sees only a holiday photograph.

The contrast with encryption is the point. Encrypted data is obviously encrypted; anyone inspecting it knows a secret exists and can compel or attack the key. Steganographic data does not announce itself at all.

📜 Think of it like…

Invisible ink versus a sealed safe

A sealed safe in the middle of a room advertises that something valuable is inside. Everyone can see it; the protection rests entirely on nobody being able to open it. That is encryption.

A letter about the weather, with a second message written between the lines in lemon juice, attracts no attention at all. Nobody tries to open it, because nobody suspects there is anything to open. That is steganography.

Where it breaks down: invisible ink offers no protection once someone thinks to warm the page. Steganography is concealment, not strength - which is why serious use encrypts the message before hiding it.

Tokenization

Tokenization replaces sensitive data with a meaningless substitute value - a token - while the real data is held in a separate, heavily protected vault. The token has no mathematical relationship to the original, so it cannot be reversed by computation. It can only be exchanged by an authorised lookup.

This is the standard approach for payment cards. A merchant stores tokens rather than card numbers, so a breach of the merchant's database yields nothing usable, and the merchant's systems fall largely outside the scope of card industry compliance.

🎫 Think of it like…

A coat check ticket

You hand over your coat and receive ticket 47. The ticket is worthless on its own - it is not a small coat, and no amount of study reveals anything about the coat. Someone who steals the ticket has stolen a piece of card.

Only the cloakroom's register connects 47 to your coat, and only staff can consult it. This is exactly the difference from encryption: ciphertext is derived from the plaintext and can be reversed with the key, but a token is arbitrary and reversible only by lookup.

Data masking

Masking replaces part of a value with placeholder characters, leaving just enough for the data to remain useful. A support agent sees **** **** **** 4471, which is enough to confirm which card a caller means but not enough to make a purchase.

Masking is usually irreversible in the view where it appears, and it is the standard answer for scenarios about giving staff or test environments realistic data without exposing the real values.

Telling them apart

Keys, Hardware, and Applied Practice

Cryptography fails in practice far more often through key management than through broken mathematics. Nobody breaks AES; they find the key in a configuration file committed to a public repository.

The key lifecycle

Every key passes through the same stages, and each is a place things go wrong.

StageThe risk if handled badly
GenerationWeak randomness produces predictable keys
DistributionKeys intercepted while being shared
StorageKeys in source code, config files, or repositories
RotationA key used indefinitely means indefinite exposure
RevocationA compromised key still trusted by relying parties
DestructionCopies surviving in backups after retirement

Generation deserves emphasis. Cryptographic strength assumes keys are genuinely unpredictable, so they must come from a cryptographically secure random source. Keys derived from timestamps or ordinary random number generators have been the root cause of real, large-scale failures.

Hardware that protects keys

TechnologyWhat it is
TPMA chip on a motherboard storing keys for that specific device; underpins disk encryption and boot integrity
HSMA dedicated appliance generating and storing keys for an organization at scale
Secure enclaveAn isolated processor region shielding data even from the operating system
Key management systemSoftware governing the full lifecycle across many systems

The shared principle is that the key never leaves the protected boundary. You send data in to be signed or decrypted and get the result back out; the key itself is never exposed to the operating system, the application, or an administrator.

🏦 Think of it like…

A bank teller behind armoured glass

You slide a document through the slot and it comes back stamped. The stamp never leaves the teller's side of the glass, and you never touch it. You cannot steal what you are never handed.

An HSM works this way. Software asks it to sign something; the HSM signs and returns the signature. Even an attacker with full administrative control of the server never obtains the key - the most they can do is request signatures while their access lasts, which is a bounded and detectable problem rather than a permanent compromise.

Key escrow

Key escrow places a copy of a key with a trusted third party so that encrypted data can be recovered if the original is lost. Organizations use it so that an employee's departure does not permanently orphan their encrypted files.

The tension is genuine, and the exam expects you to see both sides. Escrow protects availability - encrypted data stays recoverable - while weakening confidentiality, because a second party now holds the means to decrypt. It is the CIA trade-off from Module 1 in a specific technical form.

🔑 Think of it like…

A spare key with your solicitor

Leaving a spare with a solicitor means being locked out is an inconvenience rather than a catastrophe. It also means a second party can open your door, and your security now depends on their filing cabinet as well as your lock.

Whether that is wise depends entirely on what is behind the door and how much you trust the solicitor - which is the same judgement an organization makes about escrow.

Blockchain and the open public ledger

A blockchain is a ledger of transactions in which each block contains a hash of the previous block. Altering a past record changes its hash, which invalidates every block after it, so tampering is immediately visible. Copies are distributed across many participants, so there is no single record to corrupt.

For the exam, note what property this delivers. Blockchain provides integrity and transparency through an open public ledger. It does not provide confidentiality - on a public chain every transaction is visible to everyone.

📕 Think of it like…

A ledger every villager copies

Imagine a village where every transaction is read aloud and each of two hundred residents writes it in their own book, and each entry references the one before. Altering your record of last March's sale is pointless: 199 other books disagree, and every subsequent entry in your book no longer lines up.

Notice what this does and does not give you. Nobody can rewrite history - that is integrity. But everyone knows everyone's business, which is the opposite of confidentiality.

Every one of these is an implementation or management failure rather than a mathematical one. That is the section's central lesson, and it is the reason scenario questions rarely ask you to break a cipher and often ask you to spot a key in the wrong place.
